TOPIC 01Nelson et al., Ch. 6 · Evaluating tool needs

Evaluating Digital Forensic Tool Needs

Before buying anything, you decide what the tool must do. A tool that produces a beautiful report but cannot read the file system on your suspect drive is worthless, and a tool nobody has heard of is a gift to the opposing expert.

1.1

What to ask before you buy

Tool selection is driven by the cases you actually get. A laboratory that mostly handles employee-misconduct cases on Windows laptops needs something very different from one handling seized Android phones. The questions that decide the purchase:

QuestionWhy it decides the purchase
Which operating systems and file systems?NTFS, FAT, exFAT, ext4, APFS, HFS+, YAFFS2. A tool that cannot mount APFS cannot examine a modern Mac at all.
Can it read the image formats you receive?Raw (dd), Expert Witness (.E01), SMART (.S01), AFF, virtual machine disks.
Is it accepted in court?A widely used tool with published test results survives challenge. An obscure one invites a Daubert-style attack on reliability.
What is the total cost?Licence, annual maintenance, dongles, training and certification. Training is often the larger number.
Does it produce a usable report?The report is what the court reads. A tool whose output cannot be explained to a layman has failed at the last step.
How often is it updated?New phone models and OS versions appear constantly. An unmaintained tool goes blind within a year.
1.2

The five functions every forensics tool performs

However different two tools look, both are doing the same five things. Learn this list and you can evaluate any product on the market, including one released after you graduate.

FunctionWhat it means in practice
1 AcquisitionMaking the copy: physical and logical acquisition, disk-to-disk and disk-to-image, remote and live acquisition, write protection, and data compression.
2 Validation and discriminationHashing to prove nothing changed, then filtering — using known-good hash sets to hide the 200,000 Windows system files, and known-bad sets to flag contraband instantly.
3 ExtractionThe recovery step: data viewing, keyword searching, decompressing, carving, decrypting and bookmarking.
4 ReconstructionRebuilding what happened: disk-to-disk copy, image-to-disk copy, partition-to-partition and image-to-partition copies, and timeline building.
5 ReportingLog reports, report generators, and bookmark export — producing something a court can read.
Example — why discrimination is the function that saves the case

A 1 TB drive holds roughly four million files. Examining them one by one is impossible. You load the NSRL known-good hash set, and every unmodified Windows and Office file disappears from view — typically 60–80% of the drive gone in one step. You then load a known-bad set, and any file matching it is flagged the moment it is seen. What remains is a few thousand user-created files, which a human can actually read. Without discrimination the other four functions are unusable at real-world scale.

1.3

Software and hardware platforms, and the NTSC question

Tools come as software (command-line or GUI) and hardware (write-blockers, imagers, complete workstations). Many products are both — a hardware imager runs forensic software inside it.

One point students frequently miss: forensics workstations sometimes need to display video from a seized device, and video standards differ by region. NTSC is used in North America and Japan; PAL in most of Europe, India and Australia. A capture card that only handles one standard cannot read a tape or camera recorded in the other, so a laboratory handling international cases needs multi-standard capability.

A practical rule for the examination and the job

Use two tools wherever it matters. If a commercial suite and an independent tool both report the same finding, the finding is far harder to challenge. The defence must then argue that two independently written programs made the same mistake. This is called dual-tool verification and it costs very little once the image exists.

Digital Forensics Software Tools

Software tools split into command-line tools, which are small, fast and scriptable, and GUI suites, which are far easier to learn and produce the reports courts expect. Serious laboratories use both.

2.1

Command-line forensics tools

The oldest tools were MS-DOS command-line utilities small enough to fit on a floppy disk, and the approach survives because it is transparent — every action is a typed command you can put in your notes and repeat exactly. On Linux, the standard set is The Sleuth Kit, which is also the engine inside Autopsy.

Reading the output — the asterisk matters

In the fls listing the * marks a deleted entry. r/r means a regular file, and 8123-128-1 is the MFT entry number with its attribute identifiers. That number is what you hand to icat to extract the content. Every step is a typed command, so your notes reproduce the examination exactly — which is precisely what a court wants to hear.

2.2

GUI forensics tools

GUI suites made forensics accessible to investigators who are not Linux specialists. They bring all five functions into one window, handle multiple file systems, and generate court-ready reports. Their weaknesses are equally real: they are expensive, they consume large amounts of memory and disk, and they can create a false sense of confidence — the tool presents a tidy answer and the examiner stops asking how it was reached.

The trap in a one-click tool

A GUI suite will happily report "0 deleted files found" when the real reason is that the ingest module for that file system was never enabled. The tool is not lying; it answered the question it was configured to ask. Always check what modules ran before you report a negative finding — a negative is a finding too, and you will be cross-examined on it.

TOPIC 03Nelson et al., Ch. 6 · Hardware tools

Digital Forensics Hardware Tools

Hardware matters for one reason above all others: a write-blocker is the physical guarantee that you did not alter the original. Software can be argued about; a device that electrically refuses write commands is harder to dispute.

3.1

Forensic workstations

Type 1
Stationary workstation

A tower with many bays and adapters for every drive interface. Most capable, least portable. The laboratory's main machine.

Type 2
Portable workstation

A laptop with a built-in LCD and external bays. The compromise most field examiners actually carry.

Type 3
Lightweight workstation

A laptop with an external bay only. Easiest to carry, most limited — but it goes where the evidence is.

Buying a ready-made forensic workstation costs more but saves configuration time and comes supported; building your own is cheaper and more flexible but makes you responsible for every compatibility problem. Whichever you choose, plan for equipment to have a useful life of roughly 18 months to two years in a busy laboratory, and keep spare parts for the interfaces you meet most often.

3.2

Write-blockers — the single most important device

A write-blocker sits between the suspect drive and your workstation and allows read commands through while blocking every write command. Without one, simply connecting a drive to a running Windows machine will modify it: Windows writes a recycle bin, updates access times and may create a System Volume Information folder — all before you have looked at anything.

CategoryExamples and notes
Hardware write-blockersTableau, WiebeTech, CRU. A separate box with a drive interface each side. Preferred, because the blocking is physical and can be demonstrated to a court.
Software write-blockersRegistry settings or driver-level blocking. Cheaper, but depends on the operating system behaving — harder to defend.
Standalone imagersTableau TD-series, Logicube Falcon. Drive in, drive out, no computer needed; they hash as they copy and print a log.
AdaptersSATA, SAS, IDE, NVMe/M.2, USB-C. NVMe adapters are now the common gap in older kits.
Real example — the case lost at the first connection

An investigator connects a seized USB drive directly to a Windows laptop to "just check whether it has anything on it". Windows immediately writes a System Volume Information folder and updates last-access times. The hash of the drive is now different from the hash recorded at seizure. In court the defence asks a single question: "Can you prove the drive you examined is in the same state as the one you seized?" The answer is no. A device costing a few thousand rupees would have prevented it.

TOPIC 04Nelson et al., Ch. 6 · NIST CFTT and tool validation

Validating and Testing Forensics Software

You will be asked, under oath, how you know the tool worked. "It is a well-known product" is not an answer. Validation means having documented evidence that the tool produces correct results on data whose correct answer is already known.

4.1

Using national reference programmes

ProgrammeRun byWhat it gives you
CFTT — Computer Forensics Tool TestingNISTIndependent, published test reports for imaging tools, write-blockers, mobile extraction tools and more. Citing a CFTT report is the strongest short answer to a reliability challenge.
NSRL — National Software Reference LibraryNISTThe known-good hash set: hashes of millions of commercial software files, used to filter them out of view.
CFReDS — Computer Forensic Reference Data SetsNISTPrepared disk images with known contents, so you can test whether your tool finds what is documented to be there.
SWGDEScientific Working GroupBest-practice documents and validation guidance adopted widely by laboratories.
ISO/IEC 17025InternationalThe accreditation standard for testing laboratories; increasingly expected of forensic labs, and it requires documented method validation.
4.2

Validating a tool yourself — the procedure

National test reports cover the general case. Your laboratory still has to show the tool works in your hands, on your hardware. This is the procedure, and it is examinable.

  1. Obtain or build a reference data set whose contents you already know — a CFReDS image, or a drive you prepared yourself with a documented list of files, including deleted and hidden ones.
  2. Record the expected result. Write down, before testing, exactly what the tool should find. Deciding afterwards what counts as success is not validation.
  3. Run the tool under normal laboratory conditions, on the workstation you actually use, with the settings you actually use.
  4. Compare found against expected. Note every difference — missed files, false positives, timestamps reported in the wrong time zone.
  5. Repeat with a second, independent tool. Agreement between two tools is the practical standard of proof; disagreement tells you to investigate before you rely on either.
  6. Document and date the result, and retain it. Re-validate after every significant tool update — an update can change behaviour.
Example — validating a write-blocker in ten minutes

Hash a test drive: sha256sum /dev/sdb. Connect it through the write-blocker. Deliberately attempt a write: sudo touch /mnt/test/probe.txt — it must fail. Copy a file from the drive to prove reads work. Hash the drive again. The two hashes must be identical. Record both hashes, the date, the blocker's model and firmware version, and your name. That single page is what you produce in court if the blocker is ever questioned.

The answer to give in the witness box

Asked "how do you know your tool is reliable?", the complete answer has three parts: (1) the tool has been independently tested and the report is published by NIST CFTT; (2) we validated it ourselves against a reference set with known contents, and here is the dated record; (3) the critical findings in this case were confirmed with a second, independently developed tool.

TOPIC 05Nelson et al., Ch. 11 · Role of e-mail

Exploring the Role of E-mail in Investigations

E-mail appears in almost every kind of case. In criminal matters it supports investigations into fraud, harassment, narcotics and child exploitation; in civil and corporate matters it is the main evidence in harassment complaints, wrongful termination, breach of contract and theft of intellectual property.

5.1

Why e-mail is such productive evidence

Three properties make e-mail unusually valuable:

1
It is written down

Unlike a conversation, e-mail records the exact words, the time, and who was copied. People write things in e-mail they would never say in a meeting.

2
It exists in many copies

Sender's mailbox, recipient's mailbox, both servers, backups, mobile devices. Deleting your own copy does not delete the others.

3
It carries routing metadata

The header records every server the message passed through, with timestamps and IP addresses — information the sender usually does not know exists.

The investigator's advantage

A suspect who deletes a message from their Outlook has deleted one of at least six copies. The recipient still has it, both mail servers may retain it, the journal archive almost certainly does, and a phone that synced before the deletion holds another. In e-mail cases, the first question is not "is it recoverable" but "which copy is easiest to obtain lawfully".

5.2

Spoofing, phishing and what the investigation must establish

E-mail was designed in an era of mutual trust and has no built-in authentication of the From: line. Anyone can type anything there. This is spoofing, and it means the visible sender address proves nothing on its own.

TermWhat it isWhat the examiner looks for
SpoofingForging the From addressMismatch between the From: domain and the Received: chain; failing SPF/DKIM/DMARC results in the header
PhishingA message designed to harvest credentialsDisplay name versus actual address; link target versus link text; a reply-to on a different domain
Spear phishingPhishing aimed at one named person using real detailsEvidence of prior reconnaissance; correct internal names and project references
Business e-mail compromiseAn invoice or bank-detail change sent from a genuine or look-alike accountLook-alike domains (rn for m); mailbox rules created to hide replies; unfamiliar sign-in locations
Real example — the look-alike domain

An accounts clerk receives an e-mail apparently from the managing director, md@geethanjaIi-corp.com, approving an urgent payment to a new bank account. The domain looks correct at a glance. On examination the eighth character is a capital I rather than a lowercase l — a different domain entirely, registered eleven days earlier. The Received: chain shows the message never touched the company's mail servers, and the header records dmarc=fail. The visible From line was honest about a dishonest domain; the header is what proved it.

Example — same case, two protocols, two very different investigations

A suspect's laptop is seized. If the account was POP3, the .pst file on that laptop may hold years of mail that no longer exists anywhere else — the evidence is in your hands. If the account was IMAP and the suspect deleted the messages from their phone last night, the laptop cache may be empty and the server already purged. The protocol determines whether you examine a disk or draft a preservation request to the provider. Establish it in the first hour.

TOPIC 07Nelson et al., Ch. 11 · Investigating crimes and violations

Investigating E-mail Crimes and Violations

The central skill of this topic is reading an e-mail header. Everything the sender chose to show you can be false; the routing information added by the servers is far harder to forge.

7.1

The general procedure

  1. Obtain a copy of the message with full headers. A printout or a forwarded copy is not enough — forwarding rewrites the headers. Export the original (.eml or .msg) and hash it immediately.
  2. Examine the header from the bottom up to follow the message forward in time, noting every Received: hop, its IP address and its timestamp.
  3. Identify the originating IP address — the earliest Received: line that you can trust, i.e. the first one added by a server you have reason to believe.
  4. Check the authentication results — SPF, DKIM and DMARC verdicts recorded by the receiving server.
  5. Trace the IP address to its owner using WHOIS and a geolocation lookup, remembering that this identifies a network, not a person.
  6. Obtain the server logs through the proper legal process, to tie the IP address to a subscriber and a session.
  7. Document everything — the message, its hash, the header analysis, the lookups with the date they were performed, and the log extracts.
7.2

Reading a header, line by line

This is the skill examiners are tested on. Below is a header with the tell-tale signs of a spoofed message. Read it upwards from the bottom.

Message source — suspect_invoice.eml
1Received: from mx.geethanjali.ac.in (10.4.2.9) by MBX01.geethanjali.ac.in2        with SMTP; Tue, 10 Feb 2026 14:22:11 +0530          ← LAST hop (ours)3Received: from unknown-host (203.0.113.77) by mx.geethanjali.ac.in4        with SMTP; Tue, 10 Feb 2026 14:22:04 +0530        ← FIRST trusted hop5Received: from mail.corp-invoice.example (127.0.0.1)              ← FORGED: loopback6        by unknown-host; Tue, 10 Feb 2026 08:51:59 -0000  ← time goes BACKWARDS7Authentication-Results: mx.geethanjali.ac.in;8        spf=fail (domain does not designate 203.0.113.77 as permitted sender)9        dkim=none  dmarc=fail10Message-ID: <20260210.4471@corp-invoice.example>11From: "Accounts Dept" <accounts@geethanjali.ac.in>     ← claims to be internal12Reply-To: billing@corp-invoice.example               ← replies go ELSEWHERE13To: finance@geethanjali.ac.in14Subject: URGENT: revised bank details for February payment15X-Mailer: PHPMailer 6.8.0                            ← bulk script, not Outlook

Recreated: five independent indicators of spoofing in a single header

What this header proves, and what it does not

Five indicators, any one of which is suspicious: (1) spf=fail and dmarc=fail — the sending IP is not authorised for the claimed domain. (2) The From: claims an internal address, yet the message entered from outside at 203.0.113.77 — an internal message would never arrive through the external gateway. (3) The bottom Received: line claims to come from 127.0.0.1, the loopback address, which cannot be a real remote hop — it was forged by the sender. (4) The timestamps go backwards as you read down, which real routing never does. (5) Reply-To points to a different domain, so replies leave the organisation.

What it does not prove: who sat at the keyboard. 203.0.113.77 identifies a network interface at a moment in time. Tying it to a person needs the ISP's logs, obtained lawfully, and even then it identifies a subscriber account rather than a human being.

The commonest student error

Reading the header top down and reporting the first IP address found as the sender. The top Received: line is the last hop — usually your own mail server. Reporting your own gateway as the attacker's address is an error that will be noticed immediately.

The server is where the evidence survives after the user has tidied up. Server logs record what was sent and received even when no message remains, and mailbox databases retain deleted items long after the user believes they are gone.

Example — the log that survived the deletion

The finance officer, realising the mistake, deletes the entire thread from Outlook and empties Deleted Items. Nothing remains in the mailbox. The message trace above still shows all three messages, with times, recipients and subjects — because the log is written by the server as the mail flows, and the user has no access to it. 8.3

UNIX and Linux mail servers

On Linux the equivalents are plain text, which makes them easy to examine and easy to grep:

PathContains
/var/log/maillog or /var/log/mail.logThe transaction log — every message accepted, relayed, deferred or bounced.
/var/spool/mail/<user>The user's mbox — all messages concatenated in one text file.
/var/spool/mqueueThe outbound queue — messages not yet delivered, sometimes including ones the sender tried to recall.
/etc/mail/sendmail.cf, /etc/postfix/main.cfConfiguration — tells you what was logged, what was relayed, and where copies were sent.
Always read the configuration first

Before concluding that something was not logged, read the configuration file. Logging levels vary, and a message you cannot find may simply have been below the configured threshold. "Not in the log" and "did not happen" are different statements, and you will be asked to distinguish them.

TOPIC 09Nelson et al., Ch. 11 · Specialized tools · current releases

Using Specialized E-mail Forensic Tools

Specialised tools exist because manual examination does not scale. Reading 40,000 messages by hand is impossible; a tool that indexes them, threads the conversations and recovers the deleted ones turns weeks into hours.

9.1

What these tools do that manual examination cannot

Recover deleted messages

Reading inside the .pst or .ost structure to retrieve items the user deleted and the client no longer shows.

Index and search at scale

Keyword and regular-expression search across every mailbox at once, including attachment contents.

Rebuild conversations

Threading by Message-ID and In-Reply-To, so the exchange is read in order rather than as scattered items.

Report in a court-ready form

Exporting selected messages with their headers, hashes and a chain-of-custody record attached.

Understanding Mobile Device Forensics

A modern phone records where its owner went, who they spoke to, what they searched for, what they photographed and when they were asleep. It is a more complete record of a person's life than any computer, which is exactly why it is both valuable and legally sensitive.

10.1

Where data is stored on a mobile device

IdentifierIdentifiesWhere it is found
IMEIThe handsetDial *#06#, printed under the battery or on the SIM tray, and on the box
IMSIThe subscriberOn the SIM; links the account to the network operator
ICCIDThe SIM card itselfPrinted on the card and readable electronically
MSISDNThe telephone numberNetwork records; not necessarily stored on the SIM
Why the IMEI / IMSI distinction wins cases

A suspect claims the phone is not his. The IMEI shows the handset was the one recorded by the network at the time of the offence; the IMSI shows which SIM was in it. If the SIM was changed but the IMEI stayed constant, the network still tracked the same handset across both numbers. Swapping the SIM does not change the handset's identity — a point suspects routinely misunderstand and examiners routinely rely on.

10.2

What makes mobile forensics hard

Full-device encryption

Both iOS and modern Android encrypt everything at rest. Without the passcode, a physical copy of the flash chip is a copy of ciphertext — correct, verifiable, and useless.

The device is never still

A running phone receives messages, updates apps and rewrites databases continuously. Unlike a disk, you cannot freeze it without losing state.

Remote wipe

If the phone stays on a network, the owner can erase it from anywhere. This is why isolation is the first physical act at the scene.

Fragmentation and change

Thousands of models, two major OS families, and security patches every month. Tool support is a moving target — the version that worked last term may not work now.

The iOS 18 inactivity reboot — a change examiners had to adapt to

From iOS 18 Apple added an inactivity reboot: a device left unused for a period restarts itself, which forces it out of the reachable AFU state back into the locked BFU state. A phone seized in a useful condition can therefore become far harder to acquire simply by sitting in an evidence bag over a weekend. Seize, isolate and acquire without delay — the clock is now part of the threat model.

Understanding Acquisition Procedures for Cell Phones and Mobile Devices

Mobile acquisition has its own order of operations, and the first decision — made in the first minute at the scene — usually determines how much evidence you will ever obtain.

11.1

At the scene: isolate first, and leave the power alone

  1. Photograph the device as found, including the screen if it is lit. A visible notification may itself be evidence, and it may not survive.
  2. Do not switch it off, and do not switch it on. Powering down may drop the device from AFU to BFU and lose the keys held in memory. Powering up an off device may trigger a passcode requirement or a wipe policy.
  3. Isolate it from all networks — Faraday bag, Faraday box, or flight mode if you can reach it without unlocking. Isolation prevents remote wipe and stops new data overwriting old.
  4. Maintain power. A Faraday bag makes the phone search hard for a signal, which drains the battery quickly. Put a charger inside the bag; a device that dies in transit may reboot into BFU.
  5. Record the state — locked or unlocked, battery level, IMEI via *#06# if the screen is reachable, and whether a SIM and microSD are present.
  6. Seize the accessories — cables, chargers, the SIM tray tool, and any computer the phone syncs with. A synced desktop may hold a full backup you can read immediately.
  7. Acquire as soon as possible. Every hour increases the chance of an inactivity reboot, a battery death or a remote wipe.
BFU and AFU — the two states that decide everything

BFU (Before First Unlock) — the device has not been unlocked since it last powered on. Nearly all user data is still encrypted and unreachable. AFU (After First Unlock) — the device has been unlocked at least once since booting, so decryption keys are resident in memory and most user data is accessible to a privileged tool. A phone seized in AFU is enormously more productive than the same phone seized in BFU, and the only thing separating the two states is a reboot. This is the entire reason the rules above insist on not touching the power and on acquiring quickly.

11.2

The levels of extraction

Extraction methods form a ladder. Higher levels recover more, cost more, take longer and require greater expertise — and the top two destroy the device.

LevelMethodWhat it recoversCost / risk
1ManualScrolling the device by hand and photographing the screens. No deleted data.Free; slow; risks altering the device with every tap
2LogicalWhat the operating system will hand over through its normal interface — contacts, messages, call logs, photos.Fast and well supported; misses deleted items
3File systemThe device's files and, crucially, the SQLite databases, whose free pages often still hold deleted records.Needs privileged access; recovers much deleted data
4Full File System (FFS)The entire file system plus the decrypted keychain (iOS) or keystore (Android). The current gold standard.Cellebrite Premium or Magnet GrayKey; restricted licensing
5Physical — JTAG and chip-offA bit-for-bit copy of the flash memory.Destructive; and on a modern encrypted device it yields ciphertext unless the keys are also recovered
Why "physical is always best" is now wrong

On older phones a physical acquisition was the ideal, because the flash contents were readable. On a current iOS or Android device, everything at rest is encrypted, so a perfect physical image is a perfect image of unreadable data. Full File System extraction has replaced physical acquisition as the goal, precisely because it obtains the decrypted content and the keychain rather than the raw encrypted blocks. Stating "physical is always superior" in an examination answer is a dated position; explain the encryption reason and you will be right.

Example — the same phone, two outcomes

Scenario A. The phone is seized unlocked and in use. The officer places it straight into a Faraday bag with a charger and it reaches the laboratory three hours later, still in AFU. A Full File System extraction recovers chats, deleted SQLite records, location history and the decrypted keychain.

Scenario B. The same phone is seized, the officer presses the power button "to preserve the battery", and it reaches the laboratory in BFU. Nearly everything is encrypted. The examiner must now attempt passcode recovery with specialised equipment that may not support that model and iOS version, and may recover nothing at all.

The difference between a full case and no case was one button press at the scene.

11.4

Legal and documentation requirements

Scope applies to phones with particular force

A phone holds medical records, photographs of family, banking, religious and political material. A warrant to search for evidence of financial fraud does not authorise reading a person's entire life. Define the scope in writing, extract only what falls within it, and document the decision. Courts worldwide have become markedly less tolerant of "we imaged the whole phone and then looked". In India, electronic evidence is governed by Section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023, which replaced Section 65B of the Evidence Act and requires the accompanying certificate.

Record, at minimum: the device make, model and IMEI; its state when seized and when acquired; the isolation method used; the tool, version and extraction level; the hash of the extraction; and the name of the examiner. If the device state changed between seizure and acquisition — a reboot, a battery death — record that too, because it explains any gap in what you recovered.

The errors that cost most marks

Procedural errors
  • Connecting a suspect drive without a write-blocker
  • Reading an e-mail header from the top down
  • Reporting a forwarded copy as the original message
  • Powering a seized phone off — or on
  • Sealing a phone in a Faraday bag with no charger
  • Reporting a negative finding without checking which modules ran
Conceptual errors
  • Claiming physical acquisition is always superior on a modern phone
  • Treating the From address as proof of the sender
  • Confusing IMEI with IMSI
  • Saying "not in the log" when you mean "did not happen"
  • Treating an IP address as identifying a person
  • Imaging an entire phone under a narrowly drawn warrant
