Part A · Chapter 6

Evidence Collection and Data Seizure

Electronic evidence has none of the permanence that conventional evidence has, and it is much harder to form into a coherent argument. Part A is about getting hold of it before it disappears, and getting hold of it in a way that a court will still accept months later.

EX-01Vacca Ch. 6 · “Why Collect Evidence?” · pp. 217–218

Reasons to Collect Evidence

Collecting electronic evidence is expensive. The procedures are strict and exhaustive, the affected systems may be unusable for a long time, and everything collected still has to be analysed. So why bother? There are two reasons.

Reason 1
Future prevention

Without knowing what happened, you have no hope of stopping someone else — or the same attacker — from doing it again. It is the digital equivalent of not fixing the lock after a break-in. Collection is costly, but repeatedly recovering from the same compromise costs far more, in money and in reputation.

Reason 2
Responsibility

Two parties carry responsibility after an attack. The attacker is responsible for the damage, and evidence is the only way to bring them to justice. The victim has a responsibility to the community: what you learn can help others prevent the same attack — and you may have a legal obligation to analyse it, especially if your system was part of a larger attack.

Real example · the same door, twice

A retailer patches a breached web server but never investigates how the attacker got in. Six months later the same unpatched flaw is used again, this time to steal customer card data. One proper evidence review would have closed the door before it was walked through twice.

Real example · one breach, an industry-wide warning

Investigators trace a payment processor's breach to a flaw in a widely used point-of-sale system. Their findings are published as a security advisory, and hundreds of other retailers patch before they are targeted. That is the victim's responsibility to the community in action.

ExamTwo reasons only: future prevention and responsibility. Under responsibility, name both parties — attacker and victim — and say what each is responsible for.
EX-02Vacca Ch. 6 · “Collection Options” · p. 218

Collection Options

Once a compromise is detected you have exactly two options, and you have to choose quickly. Both have real costs. There is no universally right answer — the decision depends on the situation.

Option A — pull the system off the network

Disconnect and start collecting evidence immediately. The attack stops at once and no further damage is done from this machine.

GainStops the attack immediately; limits further damage; you can begin work straight away.
RiskYou may end up with insufficient evidence — or the attacker may have left a dead man switch that destroys evidence the moment the system detects it is offline.
Option B — leave it online and monitor

Keep the system running and watch the intruder work, gathering richer evidence over time about tools, behaviour and intent.

GainReveals the attacker's toolkit, timing and motives in real time, and may expose other compromised machines.
RiskYou may accidentally alert the intruder, who then wipes his tracks by any means necessary. You may also face liability if he launches further attacks on others from your network in the meantime.
Real example · the booby-trapped script

An investigator disconnects a compromised server the instant intrusion is detected. The attacker had planted a script that wipes the log files whenever the network interface goes down. The investigation loses its most valuable evidence in the first sixty seconds.

Real example · watching the intruder work

A security team notices unusual activity but keeps the server online, quietly logging every command the intruder types. Over three days they capture his full toolkit and identify two other compromised machines — evidence that disconnecting immediately would have destroyed.

ExamSay both options, then one gain and one risk for each. The two named risks worth marks are the dead man switch (disconnect) and tipping off the attacker plus liability (monitor).
EX-03Vacca Ch. 6 · “Obstacles” · pp. 218–219

Obstacles

Electronic crime is difficult to investigate and prosecute. Investigators have to build their case purely from whatever records survive after the transaction is finished. Electronic records are extremely — sometimes transparently — malleable, and electronic transactions face fewer limitations than paper ones. Together that produces what the textbook calls a collection nightmare.

Obstacle 1
Speed

Transactions complete in milliseconds. There is no window in which to intervene.

Obstacle 2
Location independence

Conducted from anywhere, through anywhere, to anywhere — crossing jurisdictions in one hop.

Obstacle 3
Encryption and anonymity

The content may be unreadable and the actor unidentified.

Obstacle 4
No intrinsic identity

No handwriting, no signature, no physical impression — nothing that belongs to a body.

Obstacle 5
Fragile paper trail

Any record left behind is easily modified, easily destroyed, or only ever temporary.

Obstacle 6
Self-erasing audit logs

Auditing programs may automatically destroy the records once the transaction is finished with them.

The identity gap — the part students miss

Even when the transaction is fully reconstructed, tying it to a person is hard. A password, PIN or any other electronic identifier does not prove who acted. It only shows that whoever did it either knew that identifier or could get past it. Authentication data authenticates the credential, not the human being.

Why the same technique fails when the medium changes

Paper-based transaction
Signed chequePhysical, unique ink signature

Retrieved years laterThe medium is durable and self-evidencing

Evidence survives and speaks for itself.

Electronic transaction

Routed via VPN / proxyFrom anywhere, through anywhere, to anywhere

The trail may be gone before anyone looks.

Real example · Cosmos Bank, Pune (August 2018)

Malware on the bank's ATM switch server authorised roughly ₹94 crore in cloned-card withdrawals across 28 countries in about two hours. Every obstacle fires at once: the transactions were fast and simultaneous, spread across many jurisdictions, the actors used stolen card credentials rather than any identity of their own, and the switch server's own logs were tampered with.

ExamList four to six obstacles and then add the identity-gap sentence. Finish with the textbook's conclusion: investigating electronic crime will always be difficult because data is easy to alter and transactions can be anonymous — the best you can do is follow the rules of evidence collection and be as assiduous as possible.
EX-04Vacca Ch. 6 · “Types of Evidence” · p. 219

Types of Evidence

Before you start collecting, know the categories. Get this wrong and you can spend weeks and a great deal of money collecting something that turns out to be useless.

Type 1 · strongest
Real evidence

Evidence that speaks for itself, without relying on anything else. In electronic terms, a log produced by an audit function — provided the log can be shown to be free from contamination.

Type 2 · strong if credible
Testimonial evidence

Evidence supplied by a witness. Subject to the perceived reliability of that witness, but as long as the witness is considered reliable it can be almost as powerful as real evidence. A word-processor document counts as testimonial as long as its author will state that he wrote it.

Type 3 · weakest
Hearsay

Evidence presented by a person who was not a direct witness. A document written by someone with no direct knowledge of the incident is hearsay. Generally inadmissible in court, and should be avoided.

TechCorp file note

Real: the firewall logged every connection with a timestamp and source IP the moment the intruder connected — written by a machine, untouched by human hands. Testimonial: the night-shift admin says “I was watching the dashboard and saw a login from an unfamiliar device at 2:07 AM.” She saw it herself and can be cross-examined. Hearsay: a colleague says “someone in the break room reckoned it was a former employee.” No direct knowledge — the court will not take it.

EX-05Vacca Ch. 6 · “The Rules of Evidence” · pp. 220–222

The Rules of Evidence

There are five rules of collecting electronic evidence. They describe five properties evidence must have to be useful. Miss one and everything else you did stops mattering.

Rule 1
Admissible

The most basic rule — the evidence must be able to be used in court. Failing this rule is the same as never having collected the evidence at all, except that it cost you more.

TechCorp: investigators get proper authorisation before imaging the server. Evidence grabbed without it, however damning, risks being thrown out entirely.

Rule 2
Authentic

You must be able to tie the evidence positively and relevantly to the incident. If you cannot connect it to what happened, it proves nothing.

TechCorp: a SHA-256 hash is generated the moment the drive image is taken, proving this exact copy is the one made that night.

Rule 3
Complete

It is not enough to collect evidence that shows one perspective. You collect evidence that proves the attacker's actions and evidence that could prove their innocence — exculpatory evidence.

TechCorp: the team shows the suspect was logged in at 2:07 AM, and also records everyone else with access that night and why each was ruled out.

Rule 4
Reliable

Your collection and analysis procedures must not cast any doubt on the evidence's authenticity and veracity.

TechCorp: standard documented tools — a write-blocker and verified imaging software — not improvised scripts. Standard method, reproducible result.

Rule 5
Believable

The evidence must be clearly understandable and believable to a jury. There is no point presenting a binary dump of process memory to people who cannot read it. But if you present a formatted, human-readable version instead, you must also be able to show its relationship to the original binary — otherwise the jury has no way of knowing whether you faked it.

TechCorp: the investigator shows an annotated timeline — “02:00 login, 02:07 files copied, 02:15 connection closed” — and can map every entry back to the raw data.

The do's and don'ts that follow from the five rules

Do
  • Minimise handling and corruption of original data
  • Account for any changes, and keep detailed logs of your actions
  • Comply with the five rules of evidence
  • Follow your local security policy
  • Capture as accurate an image of the system as possible
  • Be prepared to testify
  • Work fast — volatile evidence may vanish entirely
  • Proceed from volatile to persistent evidence
Don't
  • Do not exceed your knowledge — if you are out of your depth, learn first or find someone who is not
  • Do not shut down before collecting evidence
  • Do not run any programs on the affected system

Why not shut down? You lose all volatile evidence, the attacker may have trojaned the startup and shutdown scripts, plug-and-play devices may alter the configuration, and temporary file systems may be wiped. Rebooting is worse still. Until the compromised disk is finished with, it should never be used as a boot disk.

Why not run programs? The attacker may have left trojaned programs and libraries. Anything you run should be on read-only media and statically linked.

ExamMnemonic for the five: A-A-C-R-B — Admissible, Authentic, Complete, Reliable, Believable. For ten marks, give the five rules plus the do's and don'ts derived from them.
EX-06Vacca Ch. 6 · “Volatile Evidence” · p. 223

Volatile Evidence

Not all evidence lasts. Some resides in storage that needs a constant power supply; some sits in information that is continuously changing. If you do not capture it fast, it is gone for good.

TechCorp file note · 07:30, the server is still on

The attacker's active network connections and the contents of RAM exist only while the machine stays powered. The moment someone unplugs it “to be safe”, that evidence is destroyed forever — and with it the proof of exactly what was running at the time of the breach.

The order of volatility

Draw up a list of evidence sources ordered by relative volatility, and always collect the most volatile first. Tap any row to see what it costs you if you are late.

Most volatile — collect firstLeast volatile
TechCorp file note · doing it in the right order

Investigators dump RAM and capture the active connections and process table before touching anything else. Only then is the disk imaged. Once the raw data has been collected from the volatile sources, the system may be shut down.

Real example · a live banking-trojan infection

The responder plugs in a trusted USB of statically linked tools and works in exactly this order: memory, routing and ARP tables, process list, then the disk image. Pulling the power first would have looked safe and would have destroyed the injected process, the key held in RAM and the live command-and-control socket.

ExamMemorise all ten in order. Add the caveat the textbook gives: still weigh the circumstances — do not waste time extracting memory from an unimportant machine while an affected machine's secondary memory has not been examined.
EX-07Vacca Ch. 6 · “General Procedure” · p. 224

General Procedure

A four-step method for handling any piece of evidence, from the moment it is found to the moment it is shown in court. It is a very general outline — customise the details to your situation.

STEP 1IdentificationDistinguish evidence from junk data. Know what the data is, where it is located, and how it is stored — only then can you work out the best way to retrieve and store it.
STEP 2PreservationPreserve the evidence as close as possible to its original state. Any change made during this phase must be documented and justified.
STEP 3AnalysisExtract the relevant information and recreate the chain of events. Requires in-depth knowledge — make sure whoever analyses it is fully qualified to do so.
STEP 4PresentationCommunicate the meaning. It must be understandable by a layman while remaining technically correct and credible. A good presenter helps.
TechCorp file note · all four steps

Identify: separate the firewall logs, the memory dump and the files created around 02:00 from routine business documents on the shared drive. Preserve: image the drive through a write-blocker and record a cryptographic hash at the moment of copying. Analyse: correlate firewall timestamps, the RAM dump and file access times to reconstruct the login at 02:00, the browsing of the share, and the copy-out by 02:07. Present: hand the court an annotated timeline in plain language, not a raw memory dump — with a qualified presenter ready to be questioned.

ExamFour steps: Identification → Preservation → Analysis → Presentation. One sentence each. The presentation step's requirement — understandable to a layman and technically correct — is the one people forget.
EX-08Vacca Ch. 6 · “Collecting and Archiving” · pp. 224–225

Collection and Archiving

Once you have a plan of attack and you know what evidence is needed, the actual capture begins. Storage matters as much as capture, because storage affects how the data is perceived later.

Pillar A
Logs and logging
  • Run some kind of system logging function at all times
  • Keep the logs secure and back them up periodically
  • Logs are usually auto-timestamped, so a simple copy will normally do
  • Digitally sign and encrypt any important logs to protect them from contamination
  • Logs kept locally on the compromised machine can be altered or deleted by the attacker
  • A remote syslog server plus a sticky directory reduces that risk — though an attacker can still inject decoy or junk entries
  • Regular auditing only works if you have a clean snapshot taken before the compromise
Pillar B
Monitoring
  • Gather statistics, watch for irregular activity, and trace where the attacker is coming from
  • Watching logs as they are created reveals patterns you would miss reading them separately
  • What is missing from a log can itself be the suspicious fact
  • Traffic statistics define normal behaviour, so deviation becomes an early warning
  • Monitor users too: unknown users appearing is cause for closer inspection
  • Legal caution — limit monitoring to traffic and user information; leave content unmonitored unless the situation demands it
  • Display a logon disclaimer stating what monitoring is done, drafted with your lawyer

Where the log file lives decides whether the evidence survives

Local logging — vulnerable
Compromised web serverThe attacker now has root
/var/log/ on the same diskSame machine, same privileges
Attacker edits or deletes the logEvidence destroyed

Single point of failure: the log lives where the attacker lives.

Remote syslog — resilient
Compromised web serverWrite-only, one-way, over TLS
Hardened syslog serverSticky directory, append-only
Signed and encrypted copyEvidence survives the compromise

Residual risk: a remote server prevents deletion, not injection. The attacker can still add junk entries.

Real example · a bank Security Operations Centre in Hyderabad

Firewall, VPN and Active Directory logs are shipped in real time to a central SIEM over TLS, so a compromised teller workstation cannot erase its own history. The SIEM has learned the branch averages around forty logins a day; three hundred attempts at 2 a.m. from one host breaks that baseline and raises the alert. The monitoring disclaimer on the logon banner is what makes the captured traffic usable later.

Real example · WannaCry, May 2017

Organisations that logged only to the local disk lost their initial-infection record when the ransomware encrypted the whole volume, and could not establish patient zero. Those forwarding to a central log server still had the SMB connection records from before encryption — which is how the propagation path through port 445 was reconstructed.

ExamTwo pillars, then the two details that carry marks: remote syslog with a sticky directory, and the logon disclaimer. The disclaimer is a legal prerequisite, not a formality.
EX-09Vacca Ch. 6 · “Methods of Collection” · pp. 225–226

Methods of Collection

There are two basic forms of collection: freezing the scene and honeypotting. They are not mutually exclusive — you can collect frozen information after or during any honeypotting.

Freezing the scene

Taking a snapshot of the system in its compromised state.

  1. Notify the necessary authorities — police, your incident response team, your legal team
  2. Do not tell the world yet
  3. Copy all important data onto removable non-volatile media, in a standard format
  4. Collect the programs and utilities you used onto the same media as the data
  5. Create a cryptographic message digest for everything collected
  6. Compare each digest against the original for verification
Honeypotting

Creating a replica system and luring the attacker into it for further monitoring.

  • Sandboxing is the related method: limit what the attacker can do while he is still on the compromised system, so he can be monitored without much further damage
  • Plant misleading information — his response to it is a good way of determining his motives
  • Any data on the system that reveals his detection must be removed or encrypted, or he will cover his tracks by destroying it
  • Extremely resource intensive — may simply be infeasible
  • Legal issue: entrapment. Consult your lawyers

Where each one sits on the network

AttackerInternet
Firewall + redirectorDecides where hostile traffic goes
Production segmentReal customer data. Traffic identified as hostile never reaches it.
HoneypotA replica system seeded with misleading data. He is lured here; tools, timing and motives are recorded.
SandboxThe real host, but with capability limited. He stays where he is, monitored, without much further damage.

Out-of-band monitoring and logging run alongside all three, invisible to the attacker.

Real example · a compromised e-commerce server in a Hyderabad data centre

The card-payment host is imaged bit-for-bit to a write-blocked drive together with the dd and hashing binaries used, and every image gets an SHA-256 digest — that is freezing the scene. In parallel a decoy host on a segmented VLAN is seeded with fake customer records; the attacker returns, works on it for six days, and his toolkit and command history are captured — that is honeypotting. Both at once.

Real example · Honeynet Project research pots

A deliberately weak SSH service is exposed on a spare public IP. Within hours credential-stuffing bots log in, and the pot records every command, every uploaded binary and every outbound callback address — while no production machine is ever touched. Remember the two cautions: it costs real staff time, and any sign that the pot is instrumented must stay hidden.

ExamDo not present these as alternatives — the textbook says explicitly they combine. On honeypotting, the two things that must appear in your answer are the resource cost and the entrapment risk. Also distinguish honeypot (a decoy machine) from sandbox (the same machine, restricted).
EX-10Vacca Ch. 6 · “Artifacts” · p. 226

Artifacts

Whenever a system is compromised there is almost always something left behind by the attacker. These are artifacts. They are one of the important things you should collect — but you must be careful.

Code fragments
Trojaned programs
Running processes
Sniffer log files
The golden rule

Never analyse an artifact on the compromised system.

  • Artifacts are capable of anything — you must make sure their effects are controlled
  • Use a clean, isolated examination host with no network access
  • Analysis of artifacts can reveal other systems the attacker or his tools have broken into
Why artifacts are hard to find
  • A trojaned program may be identical to the original in every obvious way — same file size, same MAC times
  • Cryptographic checksums may therefore be necessary
  • Which means you need the original file's checksum
  • If you already perform regular file integrity assessments, this is not a problem

Detecting a trojaned artifact by checksum

Baseline /bin/ls — known good
File size142,144 bytes
MAC times2024-03-11 06:12:04
Owner / permsroot:root rwxr-xr-x
SHA-256a91f…4d7c
VS
Suspect /bin/ls — post-incident
File size142,144 bytes ← same
MAC times2024-03-11 06:12:04 ← same
Owner / permsroot:root rwxr-xr-x ← same
SHA-256e03b…19aa

Every obvious attribute matches. Only the cryptographic hash differs — so the suspect binary is an artifact. It is copied to a clean, isolated examination host. It is never executed on the victim machine.

Real example · a trojaned /bin/ls, and a supply chain

An attacker replaces the directory-listing binary with one that hides chosen filenames, pads the file and resets its timestamps so size and MAC times match exactly. Nothing looks wrong. Only a hash comparison exposes it. At the other end of the scale, the 2020 SolarWinds Orion compromise shipped a correctly signed, correctly sized DLL inside a legitimate product — defenders found it by comparing build hashes, not file properties. Record hashes of critical binaries at commissioning time, before you need them.

ExamFour artifact types, the golden rule, and the reason checksums are needed: file size and MAC times are attacker-controllable, the hash is not.
EX-11Vacca Ch. 6 · “Collection Steps” · pp. 226–227

Collection Steps

You now have enough to build a step-by-step guide. This is only a guide — customise it to your specific situation.

STEP 1Find the evidenceDetermine where the evidence you are looking for is stored. Use a checklist: it helps you collect, and it lets you double-check that everything is there.
STEP 2Find the relevant dataWork out which part of it is relevant to the case. Err on the side of over-collection, but work fast — do not spend hours on obviously useless data.
STEP 3Create an order of volatilityWork out the best order in which to gather it. This ensures you minimise the loss of uncorrupted evidence.
STEP 4Remove external avenues of changePrevention is better than cure. Stop anyone tampering with the evidence — but be careful, the attacker may have left a dead-man switch.
STEP 5Collect the evidenceUse the appropriate tools. As you go, re-evaluate what you have already collected — you may find you missed something important.
STEP 6Document everythingYour procedures will be questioned later. Timestamps, digital signatures and signed statements all matter. Do not leave anything out.
The structure to remember

Steps 1 to 3 are planning. Steps 4 to 6 are execution. Step 4's warning about a dead-man switch links straight back to the collection options at the start of the chapter — this is the same trap, met again at a different moment.

ExamGuaranteed question. Learn all six in order and be able to write one sentence on each.
EX-12Vacca Ch. 6 · “Controlling Contamination” · pp. 228–229

Controlling Contamination

Once the data has been collected, it must be protected from contamination.

Never work on the original
  • Originals should never be used in forensic examination — verified duplicates are used instead
  • This keeps the original data clean, and it also frees examiners to try more dangerous, potentially data-corrupting tests
  • Any such test must run on a clean, isolated host. You do not want to make the problem worse by letting the attacker's programs reach a network

Contamination control continues into analysis

Time
Building the timeline
  • Clock drift, delayed reporting and differing time zones create confusion in abundance
  • Never change the clock on an affected system
  • Record the clock drift and the time zone in use
  • Synchronise log timestamps; use GMT as the common reference point
Backups
Forensic analysis of backups
  • Use a dedicated examination host
  • Secure, clean — a fresh hardened OS install — and isolated from any network
  • You do not want it tampered with, and you do not want to send something nasty down the line
  • Mistakes here are recoverable: simply restore the backup again
Reconstruction
Reconstructing the attack
  • Correlate all the evidence — which is why accurate timestamps are critical
  • Use graphical tools, diagrams and spreadsheets
  • Include every piece of evidence, no matter how small
  • Leave one out and you may miss the whole picture
Real example · why GMT saves an investigation

One incident spans a web server in Mumbai (IST +5:30), a CDN node logging in UTC, and an attacker's VPS in Frankfurt (CEST +2:00). Normalising every timestamp to GMT turns three contradictory log sets into a single sequence, showing the file upload preceded the database dump by forty seconds — the ordering the whole causation argument rests on. One server ran 3 min 12 s fast; that drift was recorded, never corrected.

ExamThe two most quotable lines here: never change the clock on an affected system, and use GMT as the common reference point.
EX-13Vacca Ch. 6 · “The Chain of Custody” · p. 228

The Chain of Custody

A good way of ensuring that data remains uncorrupted is to keep a chain of custody: a detailed list of what was done with the original copies once they were collected. It will be questioned later, so document everything.

What a chain of custody records
  • Who found the data
  • When and where it was transported — and how
  • Who had access to it
  • What they did with it

Expect the documentation to end up larger than the data you collected. It is necessary to prove your case.

Follow one exhibit through the chain

Select a link to see its custody entry. Then try breaking one and watch what happens to the rest.

Custody log Six links, each one signed and timestamped. Break any single link and the whole chain — and the case — is compromised.
Watch the seal indicator in the sidebar.
Real example · a seized laptop in an Indian cybercrime case

The device is hash-verified and sealed at the scene, a Section 65B certificate under the Indian Evidence Act is prepared for the electronic record, and every later transfer is signed on the custody form. Defence counsel attacks exactly this paperwork: an unlogged four-hour gap between seizure and the evidence locker can be enough to have the exhibit excluded, no matter how sound the technical analysis was.

Four common causes of improper evidence collection

Poorly written organisational policies · no established incident response plan · lack of incident response training · a broken chain of custody.

If there is no evidence of a crime, there is no crime in the eyes of the law.Vacca, Chapter 6 summary
ExamDefine it as “a detailed list of what was done with the original copies once collected”, then give the four things it records, then the four common causes of improper collection. That is a full ten-mark answer.
Part B · Chapter 7

Duplication and Preservation of Digital Evidence

Digital evidence is the most easily lost evidence there is. Nothing in criminal justice is more easily damaged, corrupted or erased. Part B is about proving that the evidence is what you say it is, came from where you say it did, and has not been modified in any way since you obtained it.

EX-14Vacca Ch. 7 · “Preserving the Digital Crime Scene” · pp. 238–239

Preserving the Digital Crime Scene

The investigator has to worry about more than the suspect. The operating system, the applications, and the investigator's own actions are all capable of destroying the evidence before anyone has looked at it.

Four threats to the scene

Threat 1
Destructive processes planted by the owner

Devices and programs deliberately left behind to wipe data when triggered.

Threat 2
The operating system itself

When Windows starts it creates new files and opens existing ones as a normal process. Erased files can be overwritten, data in the swap file can be altered or destroyed, and directory entries for files are updated automatically — and file dates matter enormously as evidence.

Threat 3
Running any program on the subject computer

Criminals can modify the operating system so that executing a standard command destroys evidence — the DIR command is the textbook's example. Standard program names and familiar Windows icons can also be re-pointed at destructive processes.

Threat 4
Trusted applications

Even Microsoft Word and WordPerfect can become the enemy. Opening and viewing a file creates temporary files, which overwrite the temporary files that were there before — and potential evidence stored in those can be lost forever.

Where evidence actually hides on a disk

Allocated files
Spreadsheets, databases, word-processing files — the part the user can see.
File slack
Raw memory dumped at file close, stored past the end of allocated files.
Unallocated space
Erased files, and old application temporary files.
Windows swap file
Fragments of Internet activity and Windows work sessions.
A standard backup copies only this
Ambient data — invisible to the user, missed by a standard backup, and overwritten just by booting

Standard backup vs bit stream image backup

What gets copiedStandard backupBit stream image backup
Allocated filesYesYes
File slackNoYes
Erased / unallocated spaceNoYes
Swap fileNoYes
The rule

Your first objective, after securing the computer, is a complete bit stream backup of all computer data before it is reviewed or processed — normally before the computer is operated at all. A bit stream backup copies every bit of data on the storage device. For hard disk drives, make two such copies, and do all processing on one of them. The original evidence is preserved at all costs, because it is the best evidence.

To process a computer hard disk drive for evidence without a bit stream image backup is like playing with fire in a gas station.Vacca, Chapter 7
Real example · the helpful manager who “just checked”

Before the forensic team arrives, an HR manager powers on the suspect's laptop to see whether the files are really there. Windows rebuilds the page file, updates last-access times across the file table, and writes new temporary files over unallocated space. The deleted documents that would have proved the case are now partly overwritten — and defence counsel can argue every remaining timestamp was altered after seizure.

Real example · doing it properly

The drive is removed and attached through a hardware write blocker, so the examination workstation physically cannot write to it. Two images are produced, each with an acquisition hash recorded on the seizure form. One is sealed as the master; the second is restored to a sterile drive and all searching and carving happens there. The original is never mounted again.

ExamName the four threats, name the three ambient-data areas (file slack, erased files, Windows swap file), and state the bit stream rule with the two-copy detail. “Know your tools — you may only get one chance to do it right” is a good closing line.

Computer Evidence Processing Steps

Computer evidence is fragile by nature, and the problem is compounded by destructive programs and hidden data. There really are no strict rules — every case is different and flexibility matters. These sixteen general steps are offered as food for thought.

Tick them off as you revise. The four phases are a study aid, not the textbook's own grouping.

What each step actually means

StepThe detail that matters
1 · Shut downUsually pull the plug, or use the network's own shutdown commands. Screen images may be photographed at your option — but consider destructive processes running in the background, in memory or reachable through a connected modem, and a password-protected screen saver that may activate at any moment. Time is of the essence.
2 · Document hardwareBefore dismantling, photograph the system from all angles and label every wire, so the configuration can be restored exactly at the secure location.
3 · Transport securelySeized computers are too often stored insecurely. An unattended machine can be compromised, evidence planted, and crucial evidence destroyed. Without a proper chain of custody you cannot answer a lawyer who asks how you know nothing was planted after seizure.
4 · Bit stream backupsDo not operate the computer and do not process evidence until backups exist for all hard and floppy disks. All processing happens on a restored copy; the original is left untouched unless circumstances compel otherwise.
5 · Mathematically authenticateProves you did not alter the evidence once it came into your possession. Since 1989 agencies used a 32-bit process, accurate to roughly one in 4.3 billion — no longer accurate enough given today's processor speeds and disk capacities. A 32-bit CRC can be compromised.
6 · Document date and timeFile dates matter only if they are accurate. A clock one hour slow because of daylight saving makes every timestamp an hour wrong. Record the settings as found.
7 · Key search wordsDrives are too large to review file by file. Gather keywords from people familiar with the case, keep the list short, and avoid common words or words that form part of other words — surround those with spaces.
8 · Windows swap fileA valuable source of evidence and leads. Once took days with hex editors, now minutes with automated tools. On Windows 2000/XP/2003 it may be created dynamically and erased at shutdown, but its content can still be captured.
9 · File slackRaw memory dumps written past the end of allocated files when files are closed. Up to 1.1 billion bytes on a well-used drive, and roughly 80 times more Internet leads than the swap file.
10 · Unallocated spaceBillions of bytes of previously erased data — typically rich in word-processing and application temporary files.
11 · Keyword searchReview the output and document relevant findings. Each hit is reviewed for further keywords, which are added to the list and searched again. The process loops.
12 · File names, dates, timesCatalogue all allocated and erased files, sorted by name, size, content, creation date and last modified date. This produces a timeline of computer usage.
13 · AnomaliesEncrypted, compressed and graphic files are binary, so a text search cannot read them — manual evaluation is required. Review partitioning for hidden partitions, and check the Recycle Bin.
14 · Program functionalityRun programs where necessary to learn their purpose. A destructive process tied to a hot key or a common command can be used to prove willfulness.
15 · Document findingsRecord findings as they arise, including version numbers of every forensic program used. Be legally licensed — a defence lawyer will ask, and software piracy is a criminal violation of copyright law.
16 · Retain the softwareArchive a copy of the software alongside its output. Software is upgraded routinely and cases take years to reach trial; without the original version, results may be impossible to reproduce in court.

Emergency guidelines — if you are not trained

Don't turn on or operate the subject computer

Back it up with bit stream software first. Running the machine risks overwriting the swap file, which holds Internet activity and fragments of work sessions. To save grief, don't run the computer.

Don't call the resident “computer expert”

Without proper training even a world-class computer scientist can do the wrong thing. Investigators are routinely called in after an internal auditor or sysadmin has already tainted the evidence. Get a trained specialist — before the machine is switched on.

Don't read employee email unless policy allows it

Electronic privacy laws protect electronic communications. You are on safe ground only if policy states that all computers and stored data belong to the organisation and the employee has read that policy. Check with counsel. Do things by the book.

Real example · metadata that identified the BTK killer, Wichita 2005

After years of silence the offender sent police a floppy disk containing a Word document. Examiners recovered deleted metadata inside the file that named a church and the first name of the person who had last saved it. That is step 12 — documenting file names, dates and times — producing the lead that ended a decades-long case. Had anyone opened the file casually on an ordinary PC first, that metadata could have been rewritten.

ExamLearn all sixteen in order. The three highest-value details are step 5 (32-bit, one in 4.3 billion, no longer adequate), step 9 (1.1 billion bytes, 80× more leads), and step 16 (retain the software version).

Legal Aspects of Collecting and Preserving Digital Forensic Evidence

In simple terms, a chain of custody is a roadmap that shows how evidence was collected, analysed and preserved in order to be presented in court. Establishing it clearly is crucial because electronic evidence can be so easily altered.

A chain of custody must prove, at minimum
  1. No information has been added or changed
  2. A complete copy was made
  3. A reliable copying process was used
  4. All media was secured

Proving this chain is unbroken is the prosecutor's primary tool in authenticating electronic evidence.

Legal requirements for log evidence
  • U.S. Code Title 28, §1732 — log files are admissible as evidence if collected in the regular course of business
  • Federal Rules of Evidence 803(6) — logs that would otherwise be hearsay are admissible if collected during regularly conducted business activity
  • So log everything, all the time, and deal with the storage. Turning logging on only after an incident is suspected may render the logs inadmissible
  • Prove no tampering: digital signatures, a dedicated logging server, encrypted log files
The user's expectation of privacy

A log-on banner is the key to establishing that a user has no right to privacy on corporate systems. CERT Advisory CA-1992-19 provides model wording, to be tailored under legal guidance: it warns that activities may be monitored and recorded, and that evidence of criminal activity may be passed to law enforcement.

O'Connor v. Ortega, 480 U.S. 709 (1987)

The ruling implies that the legality of workplace monitoring depends primarily on whether employment policies authorising monitoring exist, and whether that policy has been clearly communicated. To prove communication, employees should sign a statement that they have read, understood and agreed to comply.

The evidence collection procedure

The first rule: do not rush. Tensions will be high and people will want answers quickly, but rushing loses evidence. Bring the incident-handling procedure, an evidence collection notebook, evidence identification tags, tools and media for making reliable copies, and — if counsel wants photographs — a camera.

Role
Incident coordinator

Contacts the response team, ensures every detail of the procedure is followed, assigns tasks, and acts as liaison to legal, law enforcement, management and public relations. Ultimate responsibility for the chain of custody belongs here.

Role
Notebook keeper

Records the who, what, where, when and how of the entire investigation process.

Role
Evidence collector(s)

Tags all evidence and makes the reliable copies. Keep the number of people minimal to avoid confusion, and they must be highly proficient with the copying and analysis tools.

What goes in the evidence notebook
  • Who reported the incident, with time, date and circumstances
  • Details of the initial assessment leading to the formal investigation
  • Names of all persons conducting the investigation
  • The case number, and the reasons for the investigation
  • Every system involved, with full specifications and identification tag numbers
  • Network diagrams, and the applications running on those systems
  • A copy of the policies governing access to and use of those systems
  • The administrators responsible for routine maintenance
  • Every collection and analysis step: date, time, description, who, where, and the result
  • An access control list of who had access to the evidence, and when
Rules for the notebook itself
  • A separate notebook for every investigation
  • It must not be spiral-bound
  • It must be bound so that the removal of a page is obvious
Three characteristics of a reliable copy process
  1. Meets industry standards for quality and reliability — a good benchmark is whether the software is used and relied on by law enforcement
  2. The copies must be capable of independent verification
  3. The copies must be tamperproof
Two copies, three destinations
ORIGINALSealed containerNever worked on. A simple file copy is not sufficient — a binary copy is the proper way to preserve evidence.
COPY 1Used for analysisAll examination happens here.
COPY 2Returned to the systemSo the business can resume service as quickly as possible.

Evidence must be transported under dual control, with the log recording how it was transported, who was responsible, and the date, time and route.

Storage and analysis of data

Access control cards show who entered the lab and when; video shows what they did inside. At a minimum keep an entrance and exit log. Group and store evidence by case, along with its notebook, and follow a clearly documented analysis plan. Log every analysis with the date and time, the tools used, the methodology and the results. Never leave evidence in an unsecured area — if a defence lawyer can show unauthorised persons had access, it could easily be declared inadmissible. When analysis is complete, a copy of the notebook goes to the legal team, and legal officials should provide a receipt detailing all items received into evidence.

Real example · Section 65B of the Indian Evidence Act

India's equivalent gate is the Section 65B(4) certificate. In Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer (2014) the Supreme Court held that electronic records produced without that certificate are inadmissible, overruling the more relaxed approach taken in the 2005 Parliament-attack case; Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Gorantyal (2020) reaffirmed it. Technically flawless imaging still fails without the certificate.

Computer Image Verification and Authentication

As investigators became more familiar with handling evidential computer material, a number of more or less formalised procedures evolved to maintain both the continuity and the integrity of that material. Part C examines those procedures, what they cannot yet do, and what a purpose-built protocol would look like.

EX-17Vacca Ch. 8 · opening · pp. 257–258

Computer Image Verification and Authentication

The current procedure, in use by both law enforcement and computer forensics investigators, works like this.

Copy 1
The master copy
  • Sealed in the presence of the computer owner, then placed in secure storage
  • Opened for examination only under instruction from the court, in the event of a challenge to the evidence presented
  • If the computer was seized and held by law enforcement, the computer itself is best evidence. If it was not seized, the master copy becomes best evidence
Copy 2
The working copy

All forensic analysis is performed on this copy. The master is never touched unless the court orders it opened.

At least two copies are taken of the evidential computer.

The assumption — and the gap in it

The procedure assumes that while in secure storage there can be no possibility of tampering with the evidence. But this does not protect the computer owner from the possibility that the secured evidence is tampered with. The protection runs one way only. Closing that gap is what the rest of Chapter 8 is about.

The growing practical problem

The difficulty is not the security or the appearance of the arrangement — it is the increasing size of the fixed disks found in computers. A disk of 2 gigabytes is no longer unusual, and it is common to find more than one fixed disk in a single machine. Media cost is falling only slowly and remains significant at this volume, even allowing for media reuse, and individual copies take a long time to complete. A sizeable saving in both time and expense might be achieved if an alternative method of evidential security could be arranged.

Real example · sealing the master in the owner's presence

Indian practice mirrors this closely: at seizure the imaging is done before panch witnesses, the master image and its hash are recorded, and the media is sealed with signatures across the seal. When the accused later disputes the working copy, the court can order the sealed master opened and its hash recomputed — exactly the challenge mechanism the textbook describes.

ExamDescribe the two-copy procedure, say who the master is sealed in front of and when it may be opened, define which item is best evidence in each scenario, and finish with the three pressures — disk size, media cost, copying time.

Special Needs of Evidential Authentication

A wealth of mathematical algorithms deal with secure encryption, verification and authentication. They vary in security and complexity, but all of them rely on a second channel of information — some element of the process kept secret. That is clearest in public and private key encryption, and present in other protocols too.

Why seizing the machine is not the answer

During an investigation it may be possible to seize or impound the computer system — but that risks violating the basic principle of innocent until proven guilty, by depriving a possibly innocent party of the use of their system. It should be perfectly possible to copy all the information in a way that leaves the original system untouched while still making everything available for forensic analysis.

The requirement that follows

When that is done, the courts may rightly insist that the copied evidence is protected from either accidental or deliberate modification, and that the investigating authority should prove this has been done.

It is not the content that needs protection, but its integrity.

The protection takes two forms

Form A
Not altered by even a single bit

A secure method of determining that the data has not been altered by even one bit since the copy was taken.

Form B
Genuinely the copy that was taken

A secure method of determining that the copy is genuinely the one taken at that time, and on the computer in question.

Collectively these two are referred to as the digital image verification and authentication protocol. Note what is excluded: when considering forensic copies of computer contents, encryption of the data is not the point at issue — and the provisions of the many digital signature protocols are not appropriate to the requirements of evidential authentication either.

The two questions the defence will actually ask

(A) “Prove nothing changed.” The examiner recomputes the hash over the image in open court; it matches the value on the seizure memo, so not one bit differs. (B) “Prove this is the image you took from my machine, that day.” That is answered not by the hash but by the seal, the signatures, the recorded drive serial number and the timestamped acquisition log. Two different problems, needing two different mechanisms.

The textbook's worked illustration — digital IDs and Authenticode

The chapter uses code signing to show hash-based authentication in practice. A digital ID (or digital certificate) is a form of electronic credential, issued by a trusted third party called a certification authority to establish the identity of the holder — much as a government issues a passport. It reliably links a public and private key pair to its owner. Anything signed with the publisher's private key can only be verified with the matching public key.

Where this model falls short for forensics

Digital IDs give exactly the two things identified above — source and integrity. But they need a certification authority, key issuance, key storage and key revocation. The next topic argues that the whole apparatus is unnecessary overhead for a forensic copying system.

EX-19Vacca Ch. 8 · “Practical Considerations” · pp. 264–265

Practical Consideration

Before asking how a forensic data collection system can be securely protected, it is worth setting out what such a system must do. These four requirements were chosen to reflect the experience of computer forensic investigators — other experts may argue against some or all of them.

Requirement 1
Complete and non-software specific

Thus avoiding software traps and hidden partitioning.

Requirement 2
As quick and as simple as possible

To avoid error or delay.

Requirement 3
Usable with minimum training

It should be possible for anyone to use the system without being an expert.

Requirement 4
Minimum cost and resources

Necessary expenditure only.

To meet requirements 2, 3 and 4, the digital integrity verification and authentication protocol must be tailored to suit. For the collection phase to remain quick and simple, the protocol must not add significantly to the time required for copying, nor introduce additional, possibly complex, procedures.

Rejected — the paraphernalia of digital signatures

The time and effort required to introduce links with key management agencies, trusted third parties and key distribution centres is not necessary. It would add cost and complexity with little increase in security, and it would mean that only investigators issued with a valid digital signature could complete copies. Four questions follow, and none of them has a good answer:

  • Who is to issue these?
  • Where are they to be stored?
  • How will each individual remember his or her own key?
  • How can misuse of keys be detected?
Adopted — a virtually self-contained system
  • The protocol is virtually self-contained, with no external key infrastructure
  • Obviously, a truly self-contained encryption system cannot on its own be cryptographically secure
  • So within the protocol, alternative channels of security are used instead to provide a truly secure system
  • The result costs far less in time and in consumables
Why this is an engineering argument, not a cryptographic one

Nothing here says public key cryptography is weak. The argument is that a van-based evidence copier operated by someone with two days' training is the wrong place to hang a public key infrastructure. The four rhetorical questions on the left are the best material for an exam answer on this topic.

Practical Implementation

The emphasis is on a practical application of proven technology, such that a minimum amount of reliance is placed on the technical ability of the operator or investigator. The system, not the person, guarantees the result.

During the copying process
  • Procedures are implemented to trap and handle hardware errors, mapping exceptions where necessary
  • Procedures are implemented to verify that information is copied correctly
  • Alongside the raw data of the suspect drive, a copy is also taken of the high section of conventional memory — including any on-board ROM areas — and of the CMOS contents via port access
Why it is built this way

Every one of the four practical considerations shows up here. Trapping hardware errors keeps it complete. Verifying automatically keeps it quick. Requiring nothing of the operator but a name, a password and the real date and time keeps it usable with minimum training.

What is written to each cartridge in a copy series

Reference area
  • CPU type and speed
  • Hardware equipment indicators
  • Copying drive serial number
  • Cartridge sequence number
  • Exhibit details and reference comments
  • Operator name with a unique password
  • The real date and time, as entered by the operator
Bulk data — the remainder of the cartridge

The information copied from the suspect drive, on a sector by sector basis.

each block hashed as it is written

The encrypted vault
  • Holds the block hash values
  • Encrypted only at the end of each section of the copy
  • Because that happens once per section, a genuinely secure encryption algorithm is affordable

Why the combination is secure

The overall security of a computer image verification and authentication system rests in the combination of security measures, not in any one of them.

Measure 1
Block hashes with a one-time pad simulation

Hash values for each block are generated in conjunction with a one-time pad simulation. As well as providing continuity between blocks, this negates the redundancy found in disk data — long runs of zeroes, ASCII text and fixed structures. Repeat hash values are therefore avoided, and a possible birthday attack is thwarted.

Measure 2
Vault encryption at section boundaries

Because encryption of the vault occurs only at the end of each section of the copy, it can be accomplished using a genuinely secure encryption algorithm rather than a fast, weak one.

Measure 3
Sealed floppies held by both parties

Both the prosecuting and the defending parties have secure protection against evidence tampering for as long as they retain their sealed floppies. In the event of a challenge, one or both envelopes can be opened in court and verified against each other and against the cartridges.

Measure 4
Locating a tampered block

In the event of a mismatch with the cartridge, reference to the encrypted vault stored on that cartridge will show which block has been altered — or reveal that the vault itself was the thing altered.

The protection depends on none of these alone — not on how securely the copy cartridges are stored, nor on the relative security attending the storage of the floppy disks, but on the combination of all three together with the technical security of the encryption mechanisms.

Real example · the modern descendant of this design

The EnCase evidence format (.E01) does almost exactly what the book describes: a header carrying case number, examiner name, acquisition date and drive details; the disk stored in fixed-size chunks with a CRC after each chunk; and an overall acquisition hash written when the image closes. Read the textbook's reference area, block hashes and vault, and you are reading the ancestor of the file format every lab uses today.

Check Yourself

One attempt per question. Pick an answer and the explanation appears — including why the wrong options are wrong.

Q1Which of these is not one of the five rules of evidence?

The five are Admissible, Authentic, Complete, Reliable and Believable. Encryption is a protective measure, not a property evidence must have.

Q2An audit log written automatically by a firewall, shown to be free from contamination, is which type of evidence?

Real evidence speaks for itself without relying on anything else. Testimonial needs a witness; hearsay comes from someone with no direct knowledge; exculpatory describes evidence that tends to clear a suspect, which is a role rather than a type.

Q3In the order of volatility, which should be collected first?

Registers and cache sit at the top of the list because they survive for the shortest time. Disk, router configuration and network topology are the three most persistent items and come last.

Q4What is the specific risk of pulling a compromised system off the network?

The textbook names the dead man switch: a mechanism that wipes evidence the moment the system detects it has gone offline. The liability risk belongs to the other option — leaving it online while it attacks others.

Q5Freezing the scene and honeypotting are…

The text is explicit: you can collect frozen information after or during any honeypotting. Sandboxing is the related third technique — the real host, with capability limited.

Q6Two copies of /bin/ls have identical file size, MAC times and permissions, but different SHA-256 values. What does that tell you?

Size, timestamps and permissions are all attacker-controllable; the cryptographic hash is not. A differing hash with identical metadata is the signature of a trojaned binary — copy it to a clean, isolated examination host and never run it on the victim machine.

Q7Which step is not among the six collection steps?

The six are: find the evidence, find the relevant data, create an order of volatility, remove external avenues of change, collect the evidence, document everything. On freezing the scene the text says the opposite of notifying the press — inform the authorities, but do not tell the world yet.

Q8Why must the evidence notebook not be spiral-bound?

It is a tamper-evidence requirement. The notebook must be bound in such a way that it is obvious if a page or pages have been removed — and a separate notebook is used for each investigation.

Q9Why is a standard backup insufficient for forensic work?

A standard backup copies the files the file system knows about. A bit stream backup copies every bit on the device, which is the only way to capture file slack, unallocated space and the swap file — where much of the evidence actually is.

Q10In the current image verification procedure, when may the sealed master copy be opened?

The master is sealed in the presence of the computer owner and placed in secure storage. It is opened for examination only under instruction from the court, in the event of a challenge to the evidence presented after analysis of the second copy.

Q11According to the special needs of evidential authentication, what actually needs protecting?

The chapter states it plainly: it is not the content that needs protection, but its integrity. Two forms follow — proving not a single bit changed since the copy, and proving the copy is genuinely the one taken at that time from that machine.

Q12Why are block hashes generated in conjunction with a one-time pad simulation?

Disk data is highly redundant — long runs of zeroes, ASCII text and fixed structures. Hashing it naively produces repeated hash values, which invites a birthday attack. The one-time pad simulation removes that redundancy and also provides continuity between blocks.
