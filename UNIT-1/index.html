Part A · Chapter 1

Digital Forensics Fundamentals

Computer crime is widespread and has infiltrated areas unimaginable a few years ago. Part A covers what the discipline is, who its evidence serves, what a forensics professional actually does for a client, and the eight steps a specialist takes on every machine.

EX-01Vacca Ch. 1 · pp. 5–8, 19–23

Digital Forensics Defined

Digital forensics is the discovery, analysis and reconstruction of evidence extracted from any element of computer systems, computer networks, computer media and computer peripherals that allows investigators to solve a crime.

The objective

To recover, analyse and present computer-based material in such a way that it is useable as evidence in a court of law.

That last phrase is the whole game. It is essential that none of the equipment or procedures used during the examination obviate it.

The priority
Accuracy, not speed

Digital forensics is concerned primarily with forensic procedures, rules of evidence and legal processes. It is only secondarily concerned with computers. So in contrast to every other area of computing, where speed is the main concern, here the absolute priority is accuracy. You complete work as efficiently as possible — as fast as possible without sacrificing accuracy.

Two components
Computer forensics vs network forensics

Computer forensics gathers evidence from media seized at the crime scene — imaging storage, recovering deleted files, searching slack and free space.

Network forensics is more technically challenging: evidence distributed across large, complex networks, often transient and never written to permanent storage.

The three roles a computer can play in a crime

A computer can be the target, the instrument, or an evidence repository — and sometimes more than one at once. Knowing which role it played tells you where to look, which matters enormously when a single drive can take days to examine.

The computer is the victim

Someone broke into it, defaced it, or knocked it over. The crime happened to the machine.

So you look for: audit logs and unfamiliar programs. If the machine was the target of an intrusion, those two sources tell you how they got in and what they left behind.

Example: a college's result-portal server is defaced overnight. The web server access logs and a newly installed PHP file are the first two things an examiner pulls.

The computer is the weapon

It was the tool used to commit the offence — the “smoking gun”.

So you look for: the tooling. If it was used to hack into a network password file, the investigator knows to look for password-cracking software and password files.

Example: a laptop used to send phishing mail carries the mailing script, the target address list and the SMTP configuration.

The computer is the filing cabinet

It stores valuable information about the crime, whether or not it was involved in committing it.

So you look for: the stored material itself — documents, images, records, correspondence.

Example: a hacker uses a machine to break into another computer and steal files, then stores them locally. That one machine is now both instrument and repository.

Who can use digital forensic evidence?

Group 1
Criminal prosecutors

Use it wherever incriminating documents can be found: homicides, financial fraud, drug and embezzlement record-keeping, child pornography.

Group 2
Civil litigations

Personal and business records bearing on fraud, divorce, discrimination and harassment cases.

Group 3
Insurance companies

May mitigate costs using discovered evidence of possible fraud in accident, arson and workman's compensation claims.

Group 4
Corporations

Hire specialists over sexual harassment, embezzlement, theft or misappropriation of trade secrets and other confidential information.

Group 5
Law enforcement officials

Frequently need assistance in pre-search-warrant preparations and post-seizure handling of the equipment.

Group 6
Individuals

Sometimes hire specialists to support claims of wrongful termination, sexual harassment or age discrimination.

Problems with digital forensic evidence

It must meet the same bar as any evidence
  • Authentic
  • Accurate
  • Complete
  • Convincing to juries
  • In conformity with common law and legislative rules — that is, admissible
But it has four special problems
  • Computer data changes moment by moment
  • It is invisible to the human eye and can only be viewed indirectly, after appropriate procedures
  • The process of collecting it may change it — in significant ways. Opening a file or printing it is not always neutral
  • The technology keeps changing, so forensic processes can seldom be fixed for very long
Why the field grew so fast

A 2003 FBI survey put the average bank robbery at $6,900 and the average computer crime at $900,000. The Internet made targets far more accessible while dropping the risk to the criminal — no one gets gunned down escaping a wire transfer. Of 849 organisations surveyed, 30% claimed theft of proprietary information, 35% experienced system penetration from outside, 23% reported sabotage, and 12% claimed financial fraud.

Use of Digital Forensics in Law Enforcement

If there is a computer on the premises of a crime scene, the chances are very good that there is valuable evidence on it. And if that computer is examined — even very briefly — by anyone other than a trained and experienced specialist, the usefulness and credibility of that evidence will be tainted.

The sentence to remember

“Even if very briefly” is doing a lot of work in that rule. A single careless boot, a single double-clicked file, and a defence lawyer has something to attack. There is no such thing as a quick harmless look.

Choosing a specialist for a criminal case

Shop around, and look hard at experience

There are an increasing number of people who claim to be experts in the field. There is far more to proper forensic analysis than the ability to retrieve data, especially in a criminal case. Think about digital forensics just as you would any other forensic science, and look for a corresponding level of expertise.

Remember who is actually testifying

You are retaining an individual who will likely be called to explain what he or she did to the computer and its data. The court will want to know that individual's own level of training and experience — not the experience of their employer. Find someone who has the expertise and the ability to stand up to the scrutiny and pressure of cross-examination.

Real example · why the individual matters more than the firm

A well-known forensics company is retained for a fraud case. The imaging is technically flawless, but the junior analyst who actually did the work has eight months' experience and has never testified. Under cross-examination he cannot explain why a particular tool reported what it did. The firm's reputation does not appear in the witness box — he does.

ExamTwo parts: the tainting rule (any non-specialist examination destroys credibility) and the criteria for choosing a specialist (the individual's own training and experience, plus the ability to survive cross-examination).
EX-03Vacca Ch. 1 · pp. 9–10

Digital Forensics Assistance to Human Resources / Employment Proceedings

Forensic analysis is becoming increasingly useful to businesses, not just to police. Computers can contain evidence in many kinds of human resources proceedings — sexual harassment suits, allegations of discrimination, and wrongful termination claims.

Source 1
Electronic mail systems

People write things in email they would never consider writing in a memorandum or letter.

Source 2
Network servers

Shared drives and mailbox stores, often with months or years of backup tapes behind them.

Source 3
Individual employee computers

Local files, browser history, downloads and drafts.

But: because computer data is so easy to manipulate, if the search and analysis is not performed by a trained specialist, it could likely be thrown out of court.

The Employer Safeguard Program

The procedure: before an individual is informed of their termination, a forensic specialist should come on-site and create an exact duplicate of the data on that individual's computer.

Protects the employer

Should the employee do anything to that data before leaving, damaged or deleted data can be replaced.

Builds the employer's case

Evidence can be recovered showing the removal of proprietary information.

Defends against false charges

It equally protects the employer from allegations made by the departing employee.

Did you know…

The textbook lists what a trained examiner can establish. Read this as a list of what a workplace machine quietly remembers.

  • What websites have been visited
  • What files have been downloaded
  • When files were last accessed
  • Attempts to conceal or destroy evidence
  • Attempts to fabricate evidence
  • That the electronic copy of a document can contain text that was removed from the final printed version
  • That some fax machines contain exact duplicates of the last several hundred pages received
  • That faxes sent or received via computer may remain on the computer indefinitely
  • That email has been used successfully in criminal cases as well as civil litigation
  • That email is often backed up on tapes generally kept for months or years
  • That many people keep their financial records, including investments, on computers
Real example · the resignation nobody saw coming

A senior sales manager resigns on a Friday to join a competitor. Because the company follows the safeguard programme, his laptop is imaged before he is told. The image shows 400 client records copied to a USB device two days earlier and a draft email — never sent, but still on disk — offering the list to the competitor. Without the pre-termination image, the drive would simply have been wiped and reissued.

ExamName the three proceeding types (harassment, discrimination, wrongful termination), the three evidence sources, and then describe the Employer Safeguard Program with its timing — before the employee is informed. The timing is the point.

Digital Forensics Services

No matter how careful they are, when people attempt to steal electronic information they leave behind traces of their activities. When people try to destroy incriminating evidence, they leave behind vital clues. In both cases those traces can prove to be the smoking gun. A forensics professional does far more than turn on a computer, make a directory listing and search through files.

The eight services

01Data seizureFederal rules of civil procedure let a party inspect and copy designated documents or data compilations that may contain evidence. Your experts act as that representative, using their knowledge of storage technologies to track evidence down, and assist officials during the equipment seizure process.
02Data duplication and preservationTwo concerns: the data must not be altered in any way, and the seizure must not put an undue burden on the responding party. An exact duplicate answers both — the responding party quickly resumes business, and work happens on the copy so the original stays intact.
03Data recoveryUsing proprietary tools, experts safely recover and analyse otherwise inaccessible evidence. When a user deletes an email, traces may still exist on the storage device — inaccessible to the user, recoverable by the expert.
04Document searchesAble to search over 200,000 electronic documents in seconds rather than hours, making discovery less complicated and less intrusive to all parties.
05Media conversionSome clients need data stored on old and unreadable devices. Experts extract it, convert it into readable formats, and place it onto new storage media for analysis.
06Expert witness servicesExplaining complex technical processes in an easy-to-understand fashion, so judges and juries grasp how the evidence was found, what it consists of, and why it is relevant.
07Computer evidence service optionsVarious service levels to suit the investigation — standard, on-site, emergency, priority and weekend.
08Other miscellaneous servicesAnalysis and on-site seizure in both criminal and civil matters, analysis of company computers to determine employee activity, help preparing electronic discovery requests, comprehensive reporting, court-recognised testimony, PC and Mac platforms, fast turnaround.

The five service levels

LevelWhen they workWhat you get
StandardNormal business hoursWork continues until your critical electronic evidence is found. Clean rooms provided, and all equipment warranties remain valid.
On-siteAt your locationExperts travel to you and quickly produce exact duplicates of the media in question, then work on the duplicate — minimising disruption. They can also help federal marshals seize data.
EmergencyHighest lab priorityYour case takes precedence over all others and is worked without interruption until your evidence objectives are met.
Priority8 a.m.–5 p.m., Mon–FriDedicated experts on your case. Typically cuts turnaround time in half.
Weekend8 a.m.–5 p.m., Sat–SunWork continues through the weekend, subject to expert availability.

What else a forensics service will do for you

Recover data you thought was lost forever

Crashes, accidental deletion, reformatted disks, viruses, overwritten files, a disgruntled employee. In many instances the data cannot be found using the limited software available to most users. Where files are irreparably damaged, expertise allows recovery of even the smallest remaining fragments.

Advise you on keeping data safe

Guidance on encryption and back-up, thorough cleaning of sensitive data from systems you plan to eliminate, and a survey of information leaks — cordless phones, cellular phones, trash, employees, answering machines.

Examine a computer to find out what its user has been doing

Finding and interpreting the clues left behind, including where files have been deleted, disks reformatted, or other steps taken to conceal or destroy evidence.

Sweep your office for listening devices

Bugging devices from micro-miniature transmitters to recorders are readily available. Experts should have the equipment for thorough electronic countermeasures (ECM) sweeps.

Real example · the $286 million slush fund

The textbook's own exercise: senior officials at a Fortune 500 company discover employees operating internal slush funds totalling roughly $286 million. A forensics professional is called in — not to prove the money moved, which the accounts already show, but to establish why and to assess impact. That means data seizure, duplication, recovery of deleted correspondence, document search across the mail store, and finally expert testimony. Six of the eight services, in one engagement.

ExamThe eight services are a classic list question — learn them in order. A common trick is a “which of these is not a service” multiple choice; the fake option is usually data dump.

Benefits of Professional Forensics Methodology

Why bring in an impartial professional rather than letting your own IT team have a look? Because of what they know, what they can find that others would not think to ask for, and — above all — what they will not accidentally destroy.

Benefit 1
Transferable expertise

The impartial expert will typically have experience across a wide range of hardware and software. It helps when they know your exact system, but fundamental computer design and software implementation is often quite similar from one system to another, so experience in one operating system or application area transfers easily to a new one.

Benefit 2
Knowing what to ask for

Unlike paper evidence, computer evidence often exists in many forms, with earlier versions still accessible on disk. Knowing that those may exist, the expert identifies more possibilities that can be requested as relevant evidence during discovery.

Benefit 3
Faster on-site identification

Where disks are not actually seized or forensically copied, the expert can more quickly identify places to look, signs to look for, and additional information sources — earlier versions of memos and spreadsheets, or differently formatted versions created by other applications.

Protection of evidence — the six guarantees

This is the heart of the benefit. A knowledgeable professional ensures that a subject computer system is carefully handled so that:

  • No possible evidence is damaged, destroyed or otherwise compromised by the procedures used to investigate the computer
  • No possible computer virus is introduced to a subject computer during the analysis process
  • Extracted and possibly relevant evidence is properly handled and protected from later mechanical or electromagnetic damage
  • A continuing chain of custody is established and maintained
  • Business operations are affected for a limited amount of time, if at all
  • Any client–attorney information inadvertently acquired during a forensic exploration is ethically and legally respected and not divulged
Note the last one

The sixth guarantee is an ethical obligation, not a technical one — and it is the one students leave out. An examiner trawling a lawyer's client's mailbox will inevitably read privileged material. Respecting and not divulging it is part of the professional methodology.

Real example · the helpful sysadmin

A company's own administrator investigates a suspected data theft by copying the user's home directory to a network share and opening a few documents to check. Every file's last-access time is now today's date, the copy has none of the deleted material, and there is no record of what was touched. A professional would have imaged the disk first and never opened anything on the original — the difference between an investigation and an accident.

ExamThree benefits plus the six protection guarantees. The guarantees are the higher-value half of the answer.
EX-06Vacca Ch. 1 · pp. 7, 18

Steps Taken by Digital Forensics Specialists

The specialist completes an Evidence Identification and Retrieval Checklist, then takes eight careful steps to identify and attempt to retrieve possible evidence on the subject system. Learn these in order.

  1. Protect the subject computer systemDuring the forensic examination, from any possible alteration, damage, data corruption or virus introduction.
  2. Discover all files on the subject systemThis includes existing normal files, deleted yet remaining files, hidden files, password-protected files and encrypted files.
  3. Recover all discovered deleted filesAll of them, or as much as possible.
  4. Reveal the contents of hidden filesTo the extent possible — as well as temporary and swap files used by both the application programs and the operating system.
  5. Access the contents of protected or encrypted filesIf possible, and if legally appropriate. Both conditions matter.
  6. Analyse all possibly relevant data in special areas of a diskIncluding unallocated space (currently unused, but possibly the repository of previous relevant data) and slack space in a file (the remnant area at the end of a file, in the last assigned disk cluster, unused by current file data but possibly a site for previously created evidence).
  7. Print out an overall analysisOf the subject system and a listing of all possibly relevant files and file data. Provide an opinion on the system layout, the file structures discovered, any discovered data and authorship information, any attempts to hide, delete, protect or encrypt information, and anything else relevant.
  8. Provide expert consultation and/or testimonyAs required.
The shape of the list

Read it as a narrowing funnel: step 1 freezes the scene, steps 2–6 find progressively harder-to-reach data (visible → deleted → hidden → encrypted → slack and unallocated), and steps 7–8 turn all of it into something a court can use. Every step after the first is worthless if the first one fails.

Real example · the eight steps on one laptop

A seized laptop in an IP-theft case runs the full sequence: imaged through a write blocker (1); the file listing shows 60,000 files including 3,000 deleted entries and an encrypted archive (2); carving recovers 2,100 of the deleted files (3); the pagefile yields a fragment of a design document (4); a warrant compels the passphrase for the archive (5); slack space holds a CAD file the suspect thought he had wiped (6); the report maps every finding to its source (7); the examiner testifies (8).

ExamEight steps in order — a guaranteed question. Step 6 is where the marks hide: define unallocated space and slack space in your own words, because the examiner is checking whether you know they are different things.
Part B · Chapter 2

Types of Digital Forensics Technology

Cyber forensics focuses on real-time, online evidence gathering rather than traditional offline disk forensics. Like medical forensics, it is generally performed postmortem — but in a networked environment, examinations must happen on an almost continuous basis. Part B looks at what the military, law enforcement and business each bring to that problem.

EX-07Vacca Ch. 2 · pp. 36–38

Types of Military Digital Forensic Technology

U.S. Department of Defense cyber forensics includes evaluation and in-depth examination of data related to both the trans- and post-cyberattack periods — during the attack as well as after it.

Objective 1
Rapid discovery of evidence
Objective 2
Estimating potential impact of the malicious activity on the victim
Objective 3
Assessing the intent and identity of the perpetrator

Real-time tracking of potentially malicious activity is especially difficult when the pertinent information has been intentionally hidden, destroyed or modified in order to elude discovery.

CFX-2000 — the Computer Forensics Experiment

The information directorate's cyber forensic concepts were new and untested, so it partnered with the National Institute of Justice through the National Law Enforcement and Corrections Technology Center (NLECTC) in Rome, New York, to test the ideas and prototype tools. CFX-2000 was the result — a first-of-a-kind event representing a new paradigm for transitioning cyber forensic technology out of military R&D laboratories and into the hands of law enforcement.

The central hypothesis

That it is possible to accurately determine the motives, intent, targets, sophistication, identity and location of cyber criminals and cyber terrorists by deploying an integrated forensic analysis framework.

How the experiment ran

SETUPA realistic scenarioNLECTC assembled computer crime investigators from DoD and federal, state and local law enforcement, hosted by the New York State Police's Forensic Investigative Center in Albany.
METHODThree identical teamsParticipants were divided into three teams. Each received an identical set of software tools and identical initial evidence of suspicious activity.
TASKUncover the mazeEach team had to uncover several linked criminal activities from a maze of about 30 milestones culminating in an information warfare crime.
RESULTHypothesis largely correctIt is possible to ascertain the intent and identity of cyber criminals.

SI-FI and digital evidence bags

SI-FI — Synthesizing Information from Forensic Investigations

Developed under contract by WetStone Technologies, SI-FI was the cornerstone of the technology demonstrated. It is an integration environment supporting the collection, examination and analysis processes employed during a cyber forensic investigation.

DEBs — digital evidence bags

Secure, tamperproof containers used to store digital evidence. Investigators seal evidence inside them and use SI-FI to collaborate on complex investigations. Authorised users can securely reopen a DEB for examination, while automatic audit of all actions ensures the continued integrity of the contents.

Other tools and prototypes in the exercise collected and analysed specific features of the digital evidence, performed case management and timelining of digital events, automated event link analysis, and performed steganography detection.

The physical analogy that makes DEBs click

A police evidence bag is tamper-evident: you can see if it was opened, and every transfer is signed on the outside. A digital evidence bag does the same job for a file — sealed, openable only by authorised users, and every single action against it written to an automatic audit trail. It is the chain of custody, implemented in software.

Real example · why the military cares about the “trans-attack” period

Traditional forensics is postmortem — you examine the body after the fact. But a defence network under active attack cannot simply be switched off and imaged; it has to keep functioning. So the objectives shift to containment while the attack is live, locating the perpetrator, mitigating damage, and initiating recovery on a crippled but still-functioning network. That is the difference between military and conventional forensics in one sentence.

ExamExpand every acronym: CFX-2000, NLECTC, SI-FI, DEB. Then the three key objectives and the central hypothesis. The six things the hypothesis claims can be determined — motives, intent, targets, sophistication, identity, location — are often asked as a list.

Types of Law Enforcement Digital Forensic Technology

Digital forensics involves the preservation, identification, extraction and documentation of computer evidence stored as magnetically encoded information. Often that evidence was created transparently by the operating system, without the knowledge of the computer operator — and may be hidden from view entirely, which is why special tools are required.

What forensic software can establish

Forensic tools and methods identify passwords, logons and other information automatically dumped from memory as a transparent operation of popular PC operating systems. They can also identify backdated files and tie a diskette to the computer that created it.

The seven areas of law enforcement forensic technology

Area 1
Computer evidence processing procedures

Procedures and methodologies should conform to federal computer evidence processing standards. Procedures have also been developed for the U.S. Treasury Department, and training and certification programmes exist through the International Association of Computer Investigation Specialists (IACIS).

Area 2
Preservation of evidence

Computer evidence is fragile and susceptible to alteration or erasure by any number of occurrences. Trainees must be exposed to bit stream backup theories that ensure preservation of all storage levels that may contain evidence.

Area 3
Trojan horse programs

The need to preserve evidence before processing should be demonstrated using programs designed to destroy data and modify operating systems. Participants must be able to avoid destructive programs and traps planted by users bent on destroying evidence — programs which can also covertly capture passwords and network logons.

Area 4
Forensics documentation

Without proper documentation it is difficult to present findings — and if those findings become the object of a lawsuit or criminal investigation, documentation matters even more. Good methodology facilitates good evidence-processing documentation and good chain of custody procedures.

Area 5
File slack

Random memory dumps in hidden storage areas. This data is a source of potential security leaks regarding passwords, network logons, email, database entries and word processing documents. Trainees must demonstrate proficiency in searching file slack, documenting findings, and eliminating the security risk.

Area 6
Data-hiding techniques

Trade secrets and sensitive data can be secreted many ways — it is possible to hide diskettes within diskettes and to hide entire hard disk partitions. Data-hiding courses are only open to classified government agencies and businesses with a demonstrated need to know, because the material could be used to defeat government security review processes.

Area 7
E-commerce investigation

Net Threat Analyzer from New Technologies Inc. identifies past Internet browsing and email activity through specific computers. It analyses a computer's disk drives and other storage areas that are generally unknown to, or beyond the reach of, most general computer users — recovering activity a user believed was cleaned away.

The three named law enforcement tools

SafeBack — mirror image backup software

Used to create mirror-image (bit-stream) backup files of hard disks, or a mirror-image copy of an entire drive or partition. The photo-negative analogy: once the negative has been made, several exact reproductions can be made of the original — but unlike a photo, SafeBack image files cannot be altered or modified to change the reproduction.

  • A worldwide standard for mirror image backups since 1990, developed to requirements set by the U.S. Treasury Department and IACIS
  • Version 3.0+ maintains integrity through two separate implementations of the NIST-tested SHA-256 algorithm
  • Preserves all data on a copied disk, including inactive and deleted data
  • Keeps a date- and time-stamped audit trail of every operation
  • Checks for possible data hiding when sector CRCs do not match, and records it automatically in the audit log
  • Does not compress relevant data — deliberately, to avoid legal arguments that the evidence was altered by compression or software translation
  • Does not write to or modify the original system; should be started from a boot diskette

Two derivative modes. Verify restores from the backup but throws the data away, letting the operator confirm the backup reads without errors. Copy feeds Restore directly from Backup with no intermediate file.

AnaDisk — diskette analysis tool

Turns a PC into a sophisticated diskette analysis tool. Originally created to meet the needs of the U.S. Treasury Department in 1991, it is used primarily to identify data storage anomalies on floppy diskettes. It works at a very low level and makes maximum use of the floppy hardware.

  • Security reviews of diskettes for storage anomalies
  • Duplication of diskettes that are nonstandard or involve storage anomalies
  • Editing diskettes at a physical sector level
  • Searching for data in traditional and nontraditional storage areas
  • Formatting diskettes in nontraditional ways — used in data-hiding courses to create hiding areas by adding extra sectors and tracks
  • Scans for anomalies identify odd formats, extra tracks and extra sectors, and flag data mismatches where file extensions have been changed to hide data

The textbook notes AnaDisk's duplication capability was used with odd diskette formats in cases such as the FBI's investigation of suspected spy Robert Philip Hanssen.

CopyQM — diskette duplication software

Essentially turns a personal computer into a diskette duplicator. In a single pass, diskettes are formatted, copied and verified — useful for specialists who need to pre-configure floppy diskettes for specific uses and duplicate them.

  • Certified by the U.S. DoD for duplicating “search disks” used in classified government computer risk reviews
  • Can create self-extracting executable programs that recreate the master diskette — so once created, anyone can produce pre-configured assessment diskettes with little technical knowledge
  • Images can be password protected using built-in encryption, keeping forensic tools away from curious hands
  • Diskette images can be serial numbered, which helps when copies are shared among several lawyers or with the court
  • Copies files, file slack and unallocated storage space (erased files)
  • Does not copy all areas of copy-protected diskettes — use AnaDisk for that
Real example · why SafeBack refuses to compress

It would be trivial to make image files smaller. The tool deliberately does not, because a defence lawyer could argue that compressing and decompressing the evidence is a software translation that may have altered it. Giving up disk space to remove a line of cross-examination is a very forensic trade — and the same instinct explains the two independent SHA-256 implementations rather than one.

ExamName the seven areas, then the three tools with one distinguishing feature each: SafeBack = evidence-grade bit stream imaging with SHA-256; AnaDisk = diskette analysis and anomaly detection; CopyQM = diskette duplication. Students routinely swap the last two.

Types of Business Digital Forensic Technology

Beyond law enforcement, businesses use a parallel set of forensic tools — from covert monitoring to theft recovery — to protect assets, investigate misuse and respond quickly when incidents occur. Same discipline, tuned for corporate risk: theft, leaks and insider misuse.

Technology 1 · covert monitoring
Remote monitoring of target computers — DIRT

Data Interception by Remote Transmission (DIRT) is a stealth remote-control monitoring tool. It watches all activity on one or more target computers simultaneously from a command centre, with no physical access needed. It also lets agents remotely seize and secure digital evidence before entering suspect premises.

Real example — building a case before the raid. A corporate security team suspects an employee of leaking trade secrets. DIRT covertly monitors his activity across the network, capturing evidence of the leak before anyone touches the physical machine.

Technology 2 · document security
Creating trackable electronic documents

Intrusion-detection tools can tag electronic documents so that any unauthorised person who accesses, downloads or views them is identified, including their location. This lets security teams trace the chain of custody and chain of command of stolen files.

Real example — catching a data leak. A company plants a tagged copy of a confidential product roadmap on its file server. Weeks later the tool flags that the file was opened from an external IP address, revealing exactly who leaked it and to whom.

Technology 3 · theft recovery
Theft recovery software — PC PhoneHome

98% of stolen computers are never recovered, and corporate theft costs over $11 billion a year. PC PhoneHome is transparent theft-recovery software that secretly emails its location every time a protected laptop or PC connects to the Internet — no user action required. It works on Windows and Macintosh and is backed by a dedicated recovery service centre.

Real example — recovering a stolen laptop. A consultant's laptop is stolen from a hotel room. Days later the thief connects it to a new Wi-Fi network; PC PhoneHome silently emails its new IP address and location, letting police trace and recover the machine.

Technology 4 · training
Basic forensic tools and techniques

Workshops train investigators and corporate security personnel in core skills: tracing email to its source, cyber-law basics, cracking passwords, remote monitoring, tracking online activity, recovering hidden or deleted data, locating stolen computers, and identifying software pirates.

Real example — applying the training. After a workshop, a corporate security analyst uses email-tracing techniques to identify the true source of a threatening internal message, resolving a harassment case in-house.

Technology 5 · outsourced expertise
Forensic services available

Specialised firms provide on-site imaging of hard drives and tapes for lab analysis with minimal disruption. Services include password and file recovery, hidden and deleted file retrieval, email decryption and authentication, honeypot sting operations, and protection from hackers, viruses and worms.

Real example — suspected embezzlement. A business suspects an employee of financial fraud. An outside service images the suspect's drive on-site, recovers deleted spreadsheets and decrypted emails in their lab, and traces threatening messages back to their true source.

The pattern across all five

Notice the shape: two technologies for gathering evidence before you confront anyone (DIRT, trackable documents), one for getting hardware back (PC PhoneHome), and two for capability — training your own people, and buying in a lab when you need one. Businesses need to act quietly, then act fast.

ExamFive items, each with its named tool where the textbook gives one: DIRT, trackable documents, PC PhoneHome. Expand DIRT in full. The two statistics — 98% never recovered, $11 billion a year — are worth quoting.
Part C · Chapter 5

Digital Forensics Evidence and Capture

Everything in Part C rests on one observation: what a suspect deleted, reformatted or physically destroyed can very often be brought back. Part C covers what data recovery is, why backup keeps failing organisations, how backup's role has changed, and what a real recovery solution looks like.

EX-10Vacca Ch. 5 · Data Recovery Defined

Data Recovery Defined

Data recovery is the process in which highly trained engineers evaluate and extract data from damaged media and return it in an intact format. In forensics, what was deleted, damaged or hidden by a suspect can be reconstructed — turning discarded data into evidence.

Recovery is needed when…
  • Accidental deletion or reformatting
  • Computer virus or malware corruption
  • Fire, smoke or water damage to media
…or when
  • Physically damaged hard drives
  • Deliberate destruction by a disgruntled user
  • Files accidentally overwritten
Why it matters to the business

A system-wide data-centre failure can be catastrophic: hundreds or thousands of employees rendered unproductive, sales transactions impossible to complete, customer service disrupted. Replacing the lost data may be extraordinarily costly — if it can be replaced at all.

The data recovery workflow

STEP 1Identify data lossWhat is missing, and when did it go?
STEP 2Assess media damageLogical, physical, or both.
STEP 3Select method and toolsMatch the technique to the failure mode.
STEP 4Image and extractWork from an image, never the failing media.
STEP 5Validate and documentProve what was recovered and how.
STEP 6Present as evidenceWhere the recovery is forensic rather than operational.

Even irreparably damaged media can yield the smallest remaining fragments — recovering what a suspect believed was destroyed forever.

Six real recovery cases from the textbook

Case 1
A Dog's Dinner

Diskettes chewed by a customer's dog suffered severe physical damage. Engineers imaged both disks with only 15% sector damage, rebuilt the file allocation tables, and recovered every file.

Case 2
Credit Card Monster

A 200 kg NetWare RAID array with 18 SCSI drives failed, losing hours of unbacked-up transactions. Three drives had failed; specialists repaired them and imaged all 18 — losing just 7 sectors out of roughly 88 million.

Case 3
Flying Low

A Toshiba laptop would not boot after travelling in a plane's cargo hold. The head/disk assembly had speckle damage from being bounced around. After a headstack swap, 86 of 112 bad sectors were still readable.

Case 4
Accounts Critical

A critical tape data loss — around 35 poorly recorded tapes — had already defeated another recovery firm. Within hours, hundreds of megabytes were extracted; the full accounts dataset was recovered within a week.

Case 5
Sinking Ship

A seismic survey ship's IBM 3590 tape had a media flaw at the start, risking a costly repeat survey at sea. Engineers located the undamaged data and safely transferred it for delivery.

Case 6
All Flooded Out

A basement flood coated more than 40 tape and optical cartridges in fine silt. Each tape was cleaned in a special rig and re-cased, and all files were returned to the customer on 26 CD-ROMs.

Where data hides — and the slack space problem

Filesystems allocate space in fixed-size blocks. Ext2 filesystems typically use 1, 2 or 4 KB blocks. If a file is smaller than the block, the remaining space is wasted — that leftover is slack space, and data written there is invisible to the filesystem, invulnerable to normal disk usage, and undetectable by file integrity checkers using checksums and MAC times.

Other places investigators look — and attackers hide
  • NTFS alternate data streams — not destroyed by many file-wiping utilities that promise irrecoverable removal
  • Open-but-unlinked files on Linux — the classic UNIX method: keep a process holding the file open, then delete it. The contents stay on disk and the space is not reclaimed
  • Deleted-but-unoverwritten blocks — /bin/rm removes the directory entry, not the data
  • Swap space remnants — fragments of whatever was in memory
  • /proc memory image — if an executable erases itself, its contents can be retrieved from /proc/$PID/exe

The tool bmap exists specifically to write data into slack space, read it back, and wipe it. Forensic software will find it; the suspect probably will not.

Real example · the reformatted laptop

An employee formats a company laptop before returning it, believing the data is gone for good. A forensic examiner images the drive and, using data-recovery techniques, reconstructs the file table and recovers spreadsheets the employee tried to erase — intact enough to use as evidence.

Real example · Satyam Computer Services, 2009

In India's largest corporate fraud case, Satyam had falsified financial records for years. Investigators used data-recovery and backup-analysis techniques to retrieve deleted financial records and emails from company servers, reconstructing the true state of the accounts. Structured, documented recovery is what made the recovered material usable as evidence.

ExamDefinition first, then the causes of loss, then the hiding places. Define slack space precisely — the leftover bytes when a file is smaller than the allocated block — and say why it defeats checksum-based integrity checkers.

Data Back-up and Recovery

Information is one of the most valuable assets a business owns — yet industry experts estimate that over 56% of client/server data is not backed up on a regular basis. Understanding why is the point of this topic.

The four obstacles to backing up

Obstacle 1
Backup window

The narrow, non-production time slot available to run backups — shrinking as businesses move to 7×24 operations.

Obstacle 2
Network bandwidth

LANs and WANs often cannot carry hundreds of gigabytes of backup traffic in the time available.

Obstacle 3
System throughput

Three I/O bottlenecks: pushing data from the system being backed up, the backup server accepting data from many systems at once, and the throughput of the tape devices.

Obstacle 4
Lack of resources

Funding for data protection loses out to competing priorities such as e-commerce projects, and in-house expertise is limited.

The four key elements of successful backup and recovery

The backup server · the network · the backup window · the backup storage device(s). Note what is not on that list — “the hardware” is a distractor that appears in the textbook's own multiple-choice question.

Backup architectures — the four textbook figures

Techniques for beating the backup window

TechniqueHow it worksKey benefit

Recommended backup features

Data interleaving

To back up multiple systems concurrently, the application must write data from multiple clients to tape in an interleaved manner — otherwise clients back up sequentially, which takes far longer.

Remote backup

Off-site locations are often not backed up at all. Laptops are especially vulnerable. A backup application should be able to back up systems across a WAN or over dial-up.

Global monitoring

Backup applications need to be accessed and administered from multiple locations, supporting reporting and administration of any backup system regardless of location.

Performance

An enterprise backup application should benchmark data rates exceeding one terabyte per hour — showing that performance is limited by hardware and network, not the application.

Real example · the 600 GB database that could not wait

A company's nightly backup window shrinks as its database grows past 600 GB. Moving that across the network would take longer than the window allows. Attaching a tape device directly to the server — LAN-less backup, Figure 5.3 — lets it back up locally without saturating the network or missing the window. The architecture is the solution to the obstacle.

The Role of Back-up in Data Recovery

Three factors have changed what backup is for. Get these three and the rest of the topic follows.

Factor 1
Storage costs are decreasing

The cost per megabyte of primary online storage has fallen dramatically and continues to. As users get accustomed to immediate access to more and more information online, the time required to restore data from secondary media is found to be unacceptable.

Factor 2
Systems have to be online continuously

7×24 operations are the norm. The amount of operationally ready data is very large and constantly increasing. The dilemma: you can no longer take files offline long enough to perform a backup.

Factor 3
The role of backup has changed

It is no longer just about restoring data. Operationally ready or mirrored data does not guard against data corruption and user error. Backup now includes responsibility for recovering user errors and ensuring good data has been saved and can quickly be restored.

Careful — this is an exam trap

The textbook's own multiple choice asks which is not a factor, and the wrong options are “storage costs are increasing” and “the role of backup has not changed”. Read the direction of each statement carefully.

Conventional tape backup, and its alternative

The typical tape management system

A dedicated workstation with the front end interfaced to the network and the back end controlling a repository of tape devices. The media server runs the tape management software, can administer backup devices throughout an enterprise, and can run continuous parallel backups and restores.

The alternative: mirror everything

Physically replicate all data and keep two copies online at all times. Because primary storage costs are falling this is not as cost-prohibitive as it once was, and the data does not have to be restored — so there are no immediate-availability issues. But it has its own drawbacks.

Four issues with today's backup

Network backup creates network performance problems

Using the production network to carry backup data as well as normal user access can severely overburden busy network resources. Installing a separate backup network helps, but even dedicated backup networks may become performance bottlenecks.

Offline backup affects data accessibility

Host processors must be quiescent during backup. It is neither host-independent nor nondisruptive, so offline time must be minimised — which requires extremely high-speed continuous parallel backup. Even then you have only deferred the real problem: the time needed to restore.

Live backups affect performance

Many database vendors offer live backup, which allows access during the process. But redirection and journaling both live on the host: up to 50% of all host CPU cycles may be consumed during the backup.

Mirroring does not protect against user error

Synchronising, breaking and resynchronising mirrors is not trivial and affects data access speeds. And duplicating data after a user has deleted a critical file, or mirroring a file already corrupted by a host process, does not help at all. Mirroring has its place but cannot solve the problem by itself.

New architectures and techniques are required

  • Backup at extremely high speed, with host-processor independence of the underlying file structures
  • Recovery must be available at the file level — not the full raw image — so the most critical information can be brought back into operation first
  • The time that systems are offline for backup must be eliminated
  • Remote hot recovery sites are needed for immediate resumption of data access. Backup and mirroring are complementary, not competing technologies
  • Decouple data from its storage space, just as programs are decoupled from the memory in which they execute
  • Journal modified pages within the primary storage device, without host intervention, using memory-mapping to track all modified information
  • Set aside part of primary storage for data to be backed up — as large as the largest backup block — and use a point-in-time snapshot of changed data while the file remains in normal operation
  • Allow backup to occur directly from the primary storage area to the backup area without host intervention, eliminating both host CPU and network bottlenecks

The net result: faster user response times during live backup, normal network performance throughout, and no backup downtime. Indexing of file structures can be done in the background after a high-speed backup, so at restore time the indices allow selection at the file level.

Real example · why file-level restore matters more than backup speed

A payroll database fails at 09:00 on a pay run. A full raw image restore takes six hours and everything comes back at once. A file-level restore brings the current month's transaction file back in eleven minutes, payroll runs, and the historical tables restore quietly in the background. Same backup, completely different business outcome — which is exactly why the textbook insists recovery must be available at the file level.

ExamThree factors, four issues, and the requirement that recovery be available at the file level. The line “backup and mirroring are complementary, not competing technologies” is very quotable.

The Data Recovery Solution

Availability once meant an application was available during the week, from nine to five. Batch processing took the evenings and nights and most people did not care, because they were at home asleep. The world has changed — customers now access their accounts in the middle of the night — and everything in this topic follows from that.

Pressure 1
Shrinking expertise, growing complexity

Systems programmers, database administrators and other mainframe experts are maturing and heading for retirement. Most bright graduates have had little exposure to mainframe concepts in their coursework, much less a grasp of day-to-day requirements. Meanwhile the complex systems built over thirty years must still be monitored, managed, controlled and optimised, batch windows are shrinking to almost nothing, and backups often take place while an application is running.

Pressure 2
Failures — and where they really come from

Hardware failures were once more common than they are today; disk storage is more reliable than ever. More likely now is a simple mistake by an application programmer, system programmer or operations person. Logic errors, or the wrong update applied at the wrong time, can cause a crash or — worse — an undetected error in the database, undetected until a customer calls days later. And disasters strike without warning: flooding, tornadoes, hurricanes, earthquakes.

Pressure 3
Budgets and downtime

Fewer resources — people, processing power, time and money — to do more work than ever. The tools that could save resources themselves cost resources to obtain, implement and operate. But businesses cannot tolerate availability problems whatever the source: if an outage occurs, the company stands to lose tens of thousands of dollars an hour. You must balance the data management budget against the cost of downtime.

The response
Recovery: think before you back up

One of the most critical data-management tasks is recovering data when there is a problem, and installations spend many hours each week preparing for that possibility. Those preparations — backing up data, accumulating changes, tracking resources — need four things: evaluate your preparations, make sure all resources are available in usable condition, automate as much as possible, and make sure you have the right kind of resources.

The four planning disciplines

  1. Evaluate your preparationsProcedures were often designed years ago and may not have been maintained. Say an organisation has always taken weekly image copies at the weekend and change accumulations mid-week. If all the resources are available at recovery time, that allows a standard recovery — but if hundreds of logs must be applied, recovery may take many hours. A strategy that was adequate when designed may be dangerously obsolete given today's availability requirements.
  2. Don't let your resources fall through the cracksReal environments use combinations of batch and online image copies of various database groups, plus change accumulations, staggered through the week. How do you check that every database is being backed up, that copies are being taken as frequently as planned, that media errors have not occurred? Finding out at recovery time that a critical resource is missing can be disastrous.
  3. Automated recoveryIf the only person who understands your systems and can recover them has moved far away, you are in trouble. Automation takes the human error factor and the “think time” out of the recovery equation and makes the complexity of the environment less of a concern. With proper planning and automation, recovery is made possible, reliance on specific personnel is reduced, and the human-error factor is nearly eliminated.
  4. Make recoveries efficientMultithreading shortens the process. Recovering multiple databases with one pass through your log data saves time. Taking image copies, rebuilding indexes and validating pointers concurrently with the recovery further reduces downtime. Where downtime is costly, time saved is money in the bank.
The RECON clean-up trap

Creating recovery JCL is not as simple as modifying existing JCL to change names. In a disaster the IMS recovery control (RECON) data sets must be modified before recovery: deleting online log data sets, closing logistics supportability, deleting subsystem records. RECON backups are usually taken while IMS is up, which leaves them needing many clean-up activities first. This process often takes hours, with the system down, equating to lost money — and discovering the deficiency at disaster-recovery time is too late.

Take backups — the last step, not the first thought

After you have thought about and planned for your recoveries, it is time to execute. The goal is to back up quickly, efficiently, and usually with minimal impact to customers. If you have a large window where systems are unavailable, standard image copies are your best option — clean recovery points, easy to manage. If you need backups while systems are active, you may need only very brief outages to take instant copies, or you may have intelligent storage devices that allow a snapshot. Both methods call for tools to assist in managing resources.

The Backup and Recovery Solution (BRS) model

Image Copy

Captures point-in-time snapshots.

Change Accumulation

Consolidates updates between image copies.

Recovery Manager

Automates and synchronises multi-database restores.

Pointer Checking

Repairs and validates database structures.

Index Rebuild

Reconstructs indexes as part of the recovery pass.

Recovery Advisor

Monitors continuously that everything is actually being backed up on schedule.

Instead of restoring twenty databases one at a time from separate tapes, a Recovery Manager groups related databases and replays log and change-accumulation data in a single automated pass — turning an all-night recovery into under two hours.

Real example · Tropical Storm Allison floods XYZ Corporation, Houston

Three feet of rain floods the basement data centre, knocking out the UPS, network switches and storage devices. But the preparations were in place and tested: weekly dual image copies with the second sent off-site; nightly change accumulations consolidating updates, with secondary copies shipped off-site each morning; daily at 6 p.m. log copies dispatched to off-site storage; and ongoing Recovery Advisor jobs verifying that copies and accumulations ran on schedule. When the disaster hit, tapes were shipped to the hot site, RECONs cleaned, recovery JCL built and run — and data was restored without errors. The team executed a practiced plan rather than improvising under pressure.

Real example · the missing log file

The counter-case. A team designed its recovery plan years ago around weekly backups. When a real outage hits, they discover hundreds of logs must be applied and one archive tape is missing — turning an expected two-hour recovery into an eighteen-hour outage. Test the plan before you need it.

ExamStructure a long answer as: the three pressures → the four planning disciplines → the BRS components → a worked example. The XYZ Corporation case is the textbook's own illustration and using it will read as a well-prepared answer.

SELF-CHECKTen questions across the whole unit

Check Yourself

One attempt per question. Pick an answer and the explanation appears, including why the other options are wrong.

Q1In digital forensics, what is the absolute priority?

In contrast to every other area of computing, where speed is the main concern, here the absolute priority is accuracy. The textbook phrases the compromise carefully: as fast as possible without sacrificing accuracy.

Q2A hacker uses a laptop to break into a bank server, then stores the stolen files on that same laptop. What role or roles did the laptop play?

It was the instrument (the tool used to commit the crime) and the evidence repository (the filing cabinet holding the stolen files). It was not the target — the bank server was.

Q3Which of these is not one of the eight digital forensics services?

The eight are data seizure, data duplication and preservation, data recovery, document searches, media conversion, expert witness services, computer evidence service options, and other miscellaneous services. “Data dump” is the textbook's own distractor in this exact question.

Q4Under the Employer Safeguard Program, when should the employee's computer be imaged?

The timing is the whole point. Imaging before the employee knows means that anything they do to the data afterwards can be detected and reversed — and it equally protects the employer against false charges made by the employee.

Q5In CFX-2000, what were digital evidence bags (DEBs) used for?

DEBs are the software equivalent of a police evidence bag. Authorised users can reopen them, but every action is automatically audited, which is what preserves the integrity of the contents. Steganography detection was done by separate tools in the same exercise.

Q6Why does SafeBack deliberately avoid compressing relevant data?

It is a legal decision, not a technical one. Giving up disk space removes an avenue of cross-examination. The same instinct explains why version 3.0 uses two separate implementations of SHA-256 rather than one.

Q7What is slack space?

Filesystems allocate in fixed-size blocks — typically 1, 2 or 4 KB. A file smaller than its block leaves the remainder unused, and that remainder can conceal data invisibly to the filesystem and to checksum-based integrity checkers.

Q8Which is not one of the four obstacles to backing up?

The fourth obstacle is lack of resources. Media shelf life is a real consideration when selecting a backup storage device, but it is not one of the four named obstacles.

Q9In serverless backup (Figure 5.2), what changes compared with a shared tape library?

The backup server drops out of the data path and keeps only the index and metadata. Performance is then limited chiefly by network bandwidth — which is the bottleneck LAN-less and SAN backup go on to address.

Q10Why does mirroring alone fail as a data protection strategy?

Duplicating data after a user has deleted a critical file, or mirroring a file already corrupted by a host process, does not help — the mirror copies the mistake. Mirroring protects against hardware loss and has its place, but backup and mirroring are complementary, not competing, technologies.
